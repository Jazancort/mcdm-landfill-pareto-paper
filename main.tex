\begin{abstract}
Multi-site facility siting is a portfolio problem: several locations are chosen
jointly under a minimum separation. Practice reads multi-criteria rankings
top-down; the literature compares methods by rank agreement,
showing they differ but not which is worse. We supply a benchmark
external to the ranking. For $\Nvia$ admissible
sites on Fogo Island, Cape Verde, we enumerate all $\Ntrios$ three-site
portfolios satisfying a $\dmin$~km separation and compute the exact Pareto frontier
over siting cost, environmental impact and social safety. Analytically, under
additive aggregation, effective weights are proportional to nominal weight times
the column amplitude; exact assembly is Pareto-efficient on all instances if and
only if within-group amplitudes are constant. Empirically, on this
instance, normalisation shifts Pareto attainment by $\NormGreedyContrast$~pp under greedy
assembly (robust; $+\NormGreedyContrastKfifty$~pp at $\kappa=50$).
The pooled SAW--TOPSIS mean contrast is near zero at $\kappa=200$
($\AggContrast$\,pp, TOST $p=\TOSTp$) but rises to
$\AggContrastKfifty$~pp at $\kappa=50$; aggregation depends on weight-draw dispersion ($\kappa$).
The value of exact
assembly under vector normalisation also depends on $\kappa$: it loses
$\AsmVecSAW$~pp (SAW) and $\AsmVecTOPSIS$~pp (TOPSIS) at $\kappa=200$,
but these effects are not distinguishable from zero at $\kappa=50$.
An intervention shows that
between-group distortion never moves the portfolio off the frontier
but costs
$\RegretEoneB$~pp of value regret;
within-group distortion reduces frontier attainment
by $\EoneWloss$~pp. Separately, a criterion encoded inconsistently
between score and objectives reduces attainment from $100\%$ to $\RaspOptimal\%$.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Normalisation re-weights criteria: effective weight is proportional to $w_jA_j$
\item Exact assembly stays Pareto-efficient iff amplitudes are group-constant
\item Normalisation shifts greedy Pareto attainment by $\NormGreedyContrast$--$\NormGreedyContrastKfifty$~pp at both $\kappa$ tested
\item Aggregation and assembly under vector depend on weight-draw dispersion ($\kappa$)
\item Criterion coded differently in score and objectives alone costs $\RaspLoss$~pp
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =======================================================================

Siting a waste facility is rarely a matter of choosing one location.
Regional plans allocate several landfills at once, and the sites must be
spatially dispersed: co-locating them concentrates traffic, leachate risk
and public opposition in one area, and defeats the purpose of a
distributed network.
The decision is therefore a \emph{portfolio} problem: choose $\kk$
sites out of $N$ subject to a minimum pairwise separation, rather than
a ranking problem.

Practice, however, is organised around rankings.
The standard workflow scores candidate sites with a multi-criteria method,
reads the ranking top-down, and skips any site too close to one already
chosen.
In a review of 114 landfill siting studies, \citet{Rezaeisabzevar2020}
found TOPSIS, SAW and AHP to be the dominant tools, and normalisation
choices to be rarely reported.
\citet{EiseltMarianov2015} provide a complementary survey of mathematical
location models for municipal solid waste facilities, covering
bi-objective MIP and MCDM formulations.
The methodological literature compares these methods extensively, but
almost always by measuring agreement between the rankings they produce
\citep{Chakraborty2009,Jahan2015,Vafaei2018,Aytekin2021,Salabun2020Sym}.
Agreement statistics establish that methods disagree; they cannot
establish that any one of them is worse, because there is no external
standard against which to judge.

This paper supplies such a standard and uses it to ask which design
choice in the workflow actually matters.
For a real set of $\Nvia$ admissible sites on Fogo Island, Cape Verde
(screened from $\Ncand$ initial candidates),
we enumerate the $\Ntrios$ feasible portfolios of size three
satisfying a $d_{\min}=\dmin$~km pairwise separation constraint, and compute the
exact Pareto frontier over three group-level objectives: siting cost,
environmental impact and social safety.
Because the frontier is obtained by exhaustive enumeration rather than by
a metaheuristic, a portfolio produced by any method can be classified as
Pareto-efficient or not, with no approximation error.
We then cross SAW and TOPSIS with three normalisations and two
assembly rules in a balanced $2^3$ design, and measure how often each cell recovers an efficient
portfolio under Monte Carlo perturbation of the elicited weights;
VIKOR and PRO\-ME\-THEE~II are included under nominal weights
(Table~\ref{tab:canonical}) and in the Supplementary Material.

The answer depends more on the normalisation than on the aggregation operator.
Normalisation moves greedy attainment by $+\NormGreedyContrast$~pp at $\kappa=200$
and $+\NormGreedyContrastKfifty$~pp at $\kappa=50$.
The SAW--TOPSIS contrast is not stable: at $\kappa=200$ its pooled mean is equivalent to zero
($\AggContrast$~pp, $95\%$ CI $[\AggLo,\,\AggHi]$, TOST $p=\TOSTp$),
with cell-level effects ranging from $\AggStratumMMgrd$~pp to $+\AggStratumVecopt$~pp;
at $\kappa=50$ SAW leads by $\AggContrastKfifty$~pp.
Under vector normalisation exact assembly loses $\AsmVecSAW$~pp (SAW) and
$\AsmVecTOPSIS$~pp (TOPSIS) relative to greedy at $\kappa=200$;
at $\kappa=50$ neither effect is distinguishable from zero.
In value regret ($R$), exact assembly achieves lower regret than greedy
under both normalisations ($\RegrDiffSAWvec$~pp, SAW/vec;
$\RegrDiffTOPSISvec$~pp, TOPSIS/vec; both below the $\delta=2.5$~pp equivalence margin): the two effects run opposite.

The reason is a mechanism that operates upstream of the aggregation
operator, and it is the analytical core of the paper.
That a criterion weight cannot be interpreted independently of the scale
on which the criterion is measured has been understood since
\citet{Choo1999}, who distinguished nominal from effective weights and
argued that the latter governs the outcome.
We state this relation in closed form: in any additive aggregation, the
effective weight of criterion $j$ is $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} \propto w_j
A_j$, where $A_j$ is the amplitude of the normalised column.
Three consequences follow immediately, and none is available from the
qualitative statement.
Min--max normalisation has $A_j \equiv 1$ and is the unique
weight-preserving choice among the three standard families.
Vector normalisation has $A_j = \rho_j$, determined by the distributional
shape of the criterion, which in our data multiplies one criterion's
weight by $\PermInflation\times$ and scales another by a factor of $\UrbanRatioVec$
(\textit{urban}) without any of this appearing in the
analyst's report.
Midranks rescaled to the unit interval contract with tie mass, and every
binary criterion loses approximately half its effective weight relative
to a tie-free continuous criterion in rank space, irrespective of
class balance.

The mechanism also explains why the value of exact assembly depends on the normalisation.
Exact optimisation of an additive score is guaranteed to yield a
Pareto-efficient portfolio under the weights that score encodes.
When $w^{\mathrm{eff}}$ departs from $w$ within an objective group,
exact optimisation solves the wrong problem exactly: on Fogo, under min--max
normalisation SAW assembly is guaranteed at $100\%$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}
and TOPSIS gains $+\AsmMMtopsis$~pp at $\kappa=200$; under vector normalisation exact assembly
loses $\AsmVecSAW$~pp (SAW) and $\AsmVecTOPSIS$~pp (TOPSIS) at $\kappa=200$
(not distinguishable from zero at $\kappa=50$)
in $Q_2$, but achieves $\RegrDiffSAWvec$~pp (SAW) and $\RegrDiffTOPSISvec$~pp (TOPSIS)
less value regret ($R$); the two effects run opposite under the distorted score.
Exact assembly remains Pareto-efficient for every instance if and only
if the normalised amplitudes are constant within each objective group
(Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}): distortion between groups only relocates
the optimum along the frontier, and only distortion within a group can
move it off.
A follow-up intervention with pre-specified predictions confirms the structural prediction
(P1, guaranteed by Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}) and falsifies the two empirical ones
(P2, P3) (Section~\ref{sec:e1}); post hoc, within-group distortion alone costs $\EoneWloss$~pp, against
$\EoneVloss$~pp for the full vector distortion (the effects are not additive).
Adding integer programming on top of a ranking
\citep{BransMareschal1992,Mavrotas2006,VetscheraDeAlmeida2012}
improved Pareto attainment under min--max and reduced it under vector
normalisation on Fogo at $\kappa=200$; the benefit is conditional on the normalisation
matching the grouping of the objectives.

A third finding concerns the encoding of criteria in the score relative
to the objectives.
In our data, $\Nexcl$ of the $\Ncand$ candidates overlap protected areas
and cannot be selected.
We find that a criterion encoded differently in the score and in the
objective function, even one with moderate weight ($0.075$), reduces
$Q_2$ from $100\%$ to $\RaspOptimal\%$ under optimal assembly through
the coding inconsistency alone.
With a consistent encoding, normalising over inadmissible alternatives
costs zero pp under optimal assembly on this instance, and greedy
assembly improves by $\RefSetGreedyGain$~pp
($95\%$ CI $[\RoneTwoGrdCIlo,\,\RoneTwoGrdCIhi]$).
The loss is therefore not an inherent property of the reference set but
of the mismatch between the score's criterion definition and the evaluator's.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item A closed-form characterisation of normalisation as implicit
    re-weighting (the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff}), formalising a relation
    established qualitatively by \citet{Choo1999} and related to
    behavioural evidence on range sensitivity in \citet{vonNitzsch1993} and \citet{Fischer1995},
    with the amplitude $A_j$ identified in closed form for min--max, vector
    and rank normalisation (Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}).
    Three effects previously reported separately, namely vector-normalisation
    distortion, tie-induced contraction in rank space, and amplitude loss
    from normalising over inadmissible alternatives, become corollaries
    of one identity.
  \item A measurement of the cost of that distortion in units of Pareto
    efficiency rather than rank agreement, against an exhaustively
    enumerated frontier of $\Ntrios$ portfolios under a hard separation
    constraint; a factorial design shows that normalisation dominates the
    choice of aggregation operator, with the assembly effect on frontier
    attainment reversing sign between min--max and vector normalisation at $\kappa=200$;
    in value regret, exact assembly
    is better than greedy under vector normalisation
    ($\RegrDiffSAWvec$--$\RegrDiffTOPSISvec$~pp) and for TOPSIS under
    min--max ($\RegrDiffTOPSISmmGrdOpt$~pp,
    $95\%$~CI $[\RegrDiffTOPSISmmGrdOptLo,\,\RegrDiffTOPSISmmGrdOptHi]$~pp;
    all below the $\delta=2.5$~pp equivalence margin).
  \item A necessary and sufficient condition for exact assembly to remain
    Pareto-efficient under normalisation: amplitudes must be constant
    within each objective group (Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}),
    two complementary indices of the weight deviation (between-group
    $\mathrm{TV}_B$ and within-group $\mathrm{TV}_W$), and a follow-up intervention with
    pre-specified predictions showing that within-group distortion carries
    the frontier attainment loss, while between-group distortion imposes
    a smaller but non-zero value regret cost.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
An exact identity connecting PROMETHEE~II to rank-weighted SAW for
arbitrary tie structure is stated in Section~\ref{prop:p2} and proved in the Supplementary Material.
Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews the relevant literature.
Section~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the portfolio problem and the
reference set; Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data and the
methods; Section~\ref{sec:design} the experimental design.
Section~\ref{sec:propositions} develops the closed-form analysis,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their implications and limits.

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:litreview}

Two bodies of work bear directly on this paper: the portfolio-assembly literature,
which treats the selection of a cardinality-constrained set as a combinatorial
problem, and the normalisation literature, which documents the effect of
scale transformations on multi-criteria scores.
The review is selective; it focuses on results that are directly used or challenged
in the remainder.
% =======================================================================

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Portfolio construction from MCDM rankings}
\label{sec:lit_portfolio}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The dominant paradigm for multi-facility siting combines an MCDM score
with a combinatorial rule.
\citet{BransMareschal1992} introduced PROMETHEE~V, in which PROMETHEE~II
flows serve as coefficients of a binary programme, and produced the first
exact solution for a portfolio problem from a ranking method.
\citet{Mavrotas2006} and \citet{Mavrotas2008} extended this to integer
programmes under budget and logical constraints, demonstrating that the
quality of the resulting portfolio depends on how closely the programme
matches the method's underlying value model.
\citet{VetscheraDeAlmeida2012} formalised $c$-optimal portfolios under
PROMETHEE~II, showing that the portfolio with exactly $c$~facilities that
maximises a PROMETHEE-derived score is sensitive to the score scale.
\citet{DeAlmeidaVetschera2012} isolated this scale sensitivity:
transforming the PROMETHEE flows before insertion into the integer
programme changes the optimal portfolio, establishing that the selection
step is not scale-invariant.

Parallel work in portfolio decision analysis (PDA) approached the problem
from a different direction.
\citet{Liesio2007} and \citet{Liesio2008} proposed Robust Portfolio
Modelling (RPM), which computes portfolios that are non-dominated in a
scalar value space over a region of uncertain weights, using an
interactive linear programme.
\citet{Mild2015} combined a multiattribute value function with portfolio optimization
for infrastructure maintenance; \citet{Yang2015SMAAPO}
combined SMAA with portfolio optimisation (SMAA-PO), producing
acceptability indices for portfolios under full weight uncertainty.
\citet{Liesio2021} reviewed 148 PDA contributions and found that
cardinality-constrained portfolio selection with spatial interdependencies
between facilities remains largely unexplored.
Within PDA, \citet{Barbati2018} optimised portfolios under multiple
satisfaction levels defined jointly across criteria, a formulation that
avoids collapsing value into a single additive score; \citet{Carazo2010}
developed a multi-objective binary programme for project portfolio
selection under resource and dependency constraints, demonstrating that
combinatorial multi-objective portfolio problems are tractable in
practice; \citet{Doerner2006} further combined ant-colony optimisation
with ILP preprocessing to approximate the full Pareto front of project
portfolios directly, bypassing the ranking step entirely.
The present paper occupies a position the PDA tradition does not fill:
the three objectives $F_1, F_2, F_3$ are kept as separate partial value
functions (not collapsed into one score), and the reference set is an
exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier under a hard pairwise spatial
separation constraint.
RPM and SMAA-PO treat weight uncertainty around a single scalar;
this paper treats decomposition by objective group and measures how much
of the resulting Pareto frontier each method--normalisation--assembly
combination recovers.

\subsection{Normalisation and effective weights}
\label{sec:lit_norm}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

That a criterion weight cannot be interpreted independently of the scale
used to normalise that criterion has been known since at least
\citet{Choo1999}, who demonstrated that nominal and effective weights
diverge whenever criterion columns have different ranges after
normalisation, and argued that the effective weight is the operationally
relevant quantity.
Behavioural antecedents of this observation exist in preference
elicitation research: \citet{vonNitzsch1993} showed in a controlled
study that subjects adjust their stated weights insufficiently when
attribute ranges change, systematically under-weighting range differences
relative to what the MAVT framework requires,
and \citet{Fischer1995} documented range sensitivity as a persistent
feature of multi-attribute value models.
These behavioural results establish that the problem is not merely
notational; it reflects how decision makers actually process
weighted criteria.
In goal programming, \citet{Kettani2004} identified an analogous
double role: weights simultaneously normalise deviations to a common
scale and encode preferences, and the two roles interact whenever the
achievable range of each goal changes; the present paper shows that
the same tension arises in additive MCDM through the amplitude
identity~\eqref{eq:weff}.
\citet{Milani2005} showed empirically that different
normalisation norms produce different rankings in a gear material
selection study and recommended vector normalisation on the grounds that
it is less sensitive to the raw scale of the data.

A large subsequent literature has measured the sensitivity of rankings to
normalisation choice, principally within TOPSIS.
\citet{Chakraborty2009} introduced the Ranking Consistency Index (RCI)
and, through simulation, found vector normalisation the most internally
consistent choice for TOPSIS; \citet{Vafaei2018} corroborated this for
TOPSIS and \citet{Vafaei2022} extended the finding to SAW.
\citet{Jahan2015} surveyed 31 normalisation techniques and emphasised
that apparently minor differences can produce materially different
engineering decisions.
\citet{Aytekin2021} compared 14 techniques across 14 data structures and
found that no single technique dominates across all settings.
\citet{Wolters1995} introduced sensitivity analysis specifically for
additive MCDM methods, characterising changes in criterion evaluations
and weights needed to alter the first-ranked alternative; the amplitude
identity~\eqref{eq:weff} derived in this paper explains analytically why
normalisation choice produces those changes.

Two observations qualify this body of work.
First, every study uses ranking agreement (RCI, Spearman's $r_s$,
or Pearson correlation between ranked lists) as its performance measure,
which establishes that outcomes differ without establishing that any
outcome is better.
Second, the recommendation of vector normalisation rests on internal
consistency with respect to the TOPSIS ideal-distance score; it does not
account for the fact that distributional shape of the criterion columns
determines the effective weight (the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff}), which may
conflict with elicited stakeholder judgements.
This paper supplies the missing dimension: a comparison against an
exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier, which measures decision quality
directly.
\citet{Liao2020} developed a double-normalisation aggregation method
that combines linear and vector normalisations to reduce sensitivity to
extreme values; the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} derived below
explains analytically why such combinations alter effective weights and
quantifies the resulting distortion.

Set dependence of normalisation has also been documented for specific
methods.
\citet{GarciaCascales2012} showed that TOPSIS rankings reverse when the
alternative set changes, and recommended scales independent of the set.
\citet{Kuo2017} identified a structural failure in the TOPSIS closeness
index under vector normalisation.
\citet{OpricovicTzeng2004} traced the linear-versus-vector contrast
between VIKOR and TOPSIS to normalisation choices with consequences for
unit invariance.

\subsection{Theory of the methods}
\label{sec:lit_methods}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The four methods evaluated here are standard.
\citet{Hwang1981} formalised TOPSIS and SAW; the original PROMETHEE
formulation is due to \citet{Brans1986}.
For VIKOR we follow \citet{OpricovicTzeng2004}.
The connection between PROMETHEE~II and Borda scoring is established
by the axiomatic results of \citet{Bouyssou1992} and
\citet{Marchant1996}.
\citet{Bouyssou1992} characterised the net flow as a scoring method
subsuming Borda and Copeland when preferences are crisp;
\citet{Marchant1996} extended the equivalence to valued preference
relations, which is the setting of PROMETHEE~II.
Section~\ref{prop:p2} exploits this connection to provide an exact
algebraic identity between the rank-normalised SAW score and the
PROMETHEE~II net flow.

Rank reversal and set dependence in PROMETHEE~II have been studied by
\citet{Mareschal2008}, \citet{Dejaegere2022}, and \citet{Coquelet2024},
who formalised the mechanism by which a third alternative shifts the
flows of existing alternatives.
These results concern a different channel than the one we study: our
set-dependence effect (Section~\ref{sec:coding} and Table~\ref{tab:coding2x2}) operates through
normalisation constants, not through pairwise preference flows.

\citet{Eppe2014} proposed piecewise-linear approximations of
PROMETHEE~II net flow scores; this result is relevant to the
identity in Section~\ref{sec:propositions}.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Benchmarking MCDM methods}
\label{sec:lit_bench}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

\begin{sloppypar}
Systematic benchmarking of MCDM methods has expanded substantially.
\citet{Cinelli2020} reviewed frameworks for selecting among methods and
argued for explicit documentation of the assumptions embedded in each
choice.
\citet{Salabun2020Sym} compared TOPSIS, VIKOR, COPRAS, and
PROMETHEE~II using stochastic simulations and Spearman correlation,
confirming that methods agree moderately under standard settings but
diverge when normalisation or weighting changes.
\citet{PintoCosta2005} proposed weighted rank correlation coefficients
for comparing decision matrices; \citet{Watrobski2019} developed a
framework for method selection incorporating data complexity diagnostics.
\end{sloppypar}

All of these benchmarks use rank agreement as their criterion of
comparison.
\citet{Mulliner2016} compared SAW, TOPSIS, and COPRAS for sustainable
housing assessment and showed that sensitivity analyses across weights
produce different rankings without identifying which ranking is closest
to the decision maker's objectives.
\citet{Mohammadi2020} proposed an ensemble-ranking framework that
aggregates the outputs of different MCDM methods into a consensus ranking,
using confidence indicators to quantify disagreement.
In a portfolio context, \citet{Korotkov2021} computed optimality
thresholds to benchmark project portfolios without full enumeration;
the present paper uses the separability of the location problem to
enumerate all $\Ntrios$ feasible portfolios exactly, enabling a comparison
against a provably complete Pareto frontier rather than a threshold
approximation.
In the combinatorial optimisation literature, \citet{Guerreiro2023}
developed exact and approximate methods for Pareto front determination
using Minimal Correction Subsets; their approach targets boolean
satisfaction problems, whereas the present paper computes the full trivariate
frontier without approximation.
The present paper adds an external standard: a reference set of
Pareto-optimal portfolios computed without collapsing the three group-level objectives into a single score.

\subsection{Spatial constraints and undesirable facilities}
\label{sec:lit_spatial}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The theory of facility location under spatial separation constraints
originates in the \emph{r-separation problem}.
\citet{MoonChaudhry1984} first formalised the problem of selecting
facilities subject to a minimum pairwise distance constraint in the
operations research literature.
\citet{Current1990} reviewed the multi-objective analysis of facility
location decisions, establishing that efficiency and equity objectives
generate inherent trade-offs that single-criterion methods cannot resolve;
spatial separation constraints were identified as a key complicating factor.
\citet{Erkut1989} reviewed siting models for undesirable facilities and
noted that MCDM methods are widely used in practice but rarely compared
against exact optimisation benchmarks.
\citet{Erkut1996} proposed integer-friendly MIP formulations for
r-separation that make exact solution tractable for problems of the size
considered here.
Multi-criteria models for undesirable facility siting on networks were
characterised by \citet{Colebrook2007}, who derived efficient solutions
and exact algorithms; the present problem is defined on a spatial
surface rather than a network, and replaces the network distance with
a minimum pairwise separation constraint (great-circle distance, Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
Multi-objective facility location problems with Pareto frontier
computation appear in a range of siting contexts.
\citet{Paul2017} used the $\varepsilon$-constraint method to generate
non-dominated solutions for multi-objective emergency facility relocation
under coverage constraints; \citet{Kinay2019} demonstrated tractability
of multi-criteria location under chance constraints via vectorial and
goal-programming formulations.
These formulations treat demand allocation and capacity explicitly;
the present problem is deterministic, uncapacitated, and
includes instead a hard pairwise separation constraint that reduces the
feasible set from all $\binom{\Nvia}{\kk}$ triples to $\Ntrios$.
\citet{Kuby1987} studied the related p-dispersion problem (maximise the
minimum inter-facility distance); \citet{Church2022} reviewed obnoxious facilities location problems;
\citet{Marti2022} provided the most recent comprehensive review of
discrete dispersion maximisation from an operations research perspective.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Landfill site selection}
\label{sec:lit_landfill}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

MCDM methods are the dominant tool for landfill site selection in the
GIS-integrated literature.
\citet{Rezaeisabzevar2020} reviewed 114 studies and found that TOPSIS,
SAW, and AHP dominate, with normalisation choices rarely reported.
\citet{Korucu2012} reviewed the field critically and identified the
absence of spatial interdependencies among selected sites as a gap.
\citet{Spigolon2018} combined MCDA with mixed-integer quadratic
programming for multi-facility selection, but without comparing methods.
\citet{Demesouka2019} applied spatial MCDM with robustness analysis to
landfill siting in Greece, combining GIS-based scoring with ELECTRE TRI
to handle weight uncertainty across multiple stakeholder scenarios; that
study evaluates suitability class assignments rather than portfolio
efficiency against a Pareto frontier.
\citet{EiseltMarianov2014} were, to our knowledge, the only prior study
apart from \citet{AzancortNeto2026} to
compute an exact Pareto frontier for a landfill siting problem; they use
a bi-objective MIP without any MCDM ranking component, and the frontier
is bivariate rather than trivariate.
\citet{AzancortNeto2026} provide the spatial dataset used in the present
paper: $\Ncand$ candidate centroids on Fogo Island validated against a
complete exhaustive search of the feasible combinatorial space, with
AHP-derived weights and an exact Pareto frontier serving as the
multi-objective ground truth.
Precursor analyses of this dataset appear in \citet{AzancortNeto2021}
(decision-tree classification of site suitability) and \citet{Costa2020}
(mathematical and computational modelling of the AHP criteria matrix).
No study in this literature has compared ranking-based MCDM methods
against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier under a hard spatial
separation constraint while systematically varying aggregation,
normalisation, and assembly strategy.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The present contribution}
\label{sec:lit_gap}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

Drawing together the observations above, the gap is as follows.
The PDA tradition computes exact portfolios in a scalar value space but
does not decompose objectives into groups or impose hard spatial
separation.
The normalisation literature documents sensitivity of rankings to
normalisation choice but measures it by internal agreement, not by an
external quality standard.
The benchmarking literature compares methods by rank agreement.
The landfill literature uses MCDM rankings without comparing them to any
optimality benchmark.

The present paper addresses this gap by: (i)~enumerating the exact Pareto
frontier of $\Ntrios$ portfolios over three group-level partial value
functions under $r$-separation; (ii)~evaluating SAW and TOPSIS in a balanced $2^3$ design (normalisation
$\times$ assembly) against that frontier; VIKOR and PROMETHEE~II are
included under nominal weights and in the Supplementary Material;
and (iii)~providing a closed-form account of the mechanism by which
normalisation implicitly re-weights criteria, expressed in terms
established by \citet{Choo1999} and measured in terms of Pareto
efficiency loss.

In the literature reviewed (the studies cited in Sections~\ref{sec:litreview}
and~\ref{sec:lit_gap}, including the 148-article PDA survey of \citet{Liesio2021}),
no prior work evaluates ranking-based MCDM portfolios against an exhaustive
Pareto frontier under hard spatial separation, crosses aggregation, normalisation,
and assembly as a
factorial, or measures the cost of normalisation choice in units of
Pareto efficiency.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

This section defines the siting problem formally and introduces the three evaluation
axes, the separation constraint, and the Pareto frontier against which all
method--normalisation--assembly combinations are subsequently measured.

\subsection{Candidate sites and the separation constraint}

Let $S=\{1,\dots,N\}$ index candidate sites, each described by $m=11$
geospatial criteria.
A \emph{portfolio} is a subset $t\subseteq S$ with $|t|=\kk$.
Portfolios must satisfy a hard pairwise separation constraint
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rsep}
  d(u,v) \;\ge\; d_{\min}=\dmin\;\text{km}
  \qquad \forall\, u,v\in t,\; u\neq v,
\end{equation}
where $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the great-circle distance.
Constraint~\eqref{eq:rsep} is the $r$-separation condition of
\citet{Erkut1996}, whose network antecedent is \citet{MoonChaudhry1984}.
We emphasise that dispersion enters here as a \emph{constraint}, not an
objective: the $p$-dispersion literature \citep{Kuby1987,Marti2022}
maximises the minimum pairwise distance, whereas~\eqref{eq:rsep} merely
renders non-compliant portfolios infeasible.
The feasible set is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:T}
  \Tset = \bigl\{\,t\subseteq S \;:\; |t|=\kk,\ t \text{ satisfies }
    \eqref{eq:rsep}\,\bigr\},
  \qquad |\Tset| = \Ntrios.
\end{equation}
Throughout, $S$ is the set of $\Nvia$ admissible sites
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}); the $\Nexcl$ excluded candidates never enter
a portfolio in either reference space and affect only the normalisation
constants.

\subsection{Three partial value functions}

The $m$ criteria are partitioned into three groups reflecting the
decision dimensions of landfill siting: siting cost ($G_1$),
environmental impact ($G_2$), and social safety ($G_3$)
(Table~\ref{tab:criteria}).
Let $c_j(i)\in[0,1]$ be the partial value of site~$i$ on criterion~$j$,
built so that larger values denote a less desirable outcome for $G_1$ and
$G_2$ (in $G_2$, distance criteria enter as $1-\hat{x}_j$
because proximity to sensitive receptors is a hazard, so a short distance is a high cost;
$G_1$ criteria are already oriented so that larger values denote greater cost or severity),
and a more desirable outcome for
$G_3$, and let $w_j$ be the criterion weight.
The three objectives therefore have mixed senses:
\begin{align}
  F_1(t) &= \sum_{i\in t}\sum_{j\in G_1} w_j\,c_j(i),
    && \text{(cost, min)} \label{eq:F1}\\
  F_2(t) &= \sum_{i\in t}\sum_{j\in G_2} w_j\,c_j(i),
    && \text{(env.\ impact, min)} \label{eq:F2}\\
  F_3(t) &= \sum_{i\in t}\sum_{j\in G_3} w_j\,c_j(i),
    && \text{(social safety, max)} \label{eq:F3}
\end{align}
and we write $\mathbf{F}(t;w)=(F_1,F_2,F_3)$ to make the weight
dependence explicit, since it is central to Section~\ref{sec:design}.

Table~\ref{tab:criteria} records the group, sense, and SAW term for each criterion.
For \textit{aspect}, the decision-matrix encoding ($b_\mathrm{asp}=1$ for
SE-facing) and the objective encoding ($c_\mathrm{asp}=1-b_\mathrm{asp}$)
are equivalent under min--max normalisation (top-100 rank agreement
$=1.000$) but differ under vector normalisation by a factor of
$\sqrt{n_0/n_1}=\AspectAmplitudeRatio$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:order}); score and
objective use the decision-matrix encoding throughout
(a score--evaluator coding mismatch is analysed in Section~\ref{sec:coding}).
Amplitudes in Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} are measured on the decision
matrix, with $n_1=94$ SE-facing sites among the $\Nvia$ admissible sites.
The SAW method produces the oriented score
$s(a)=\sum_{j\in G_1\cup G_2} w_j(1-c_j(a))+\sum_{j\in G_3} w_j c_j(a)$;
Section~\ref{prop:p1} uses this relationship.

\paragraph{What the benchmark is, and is not.}
\label{rem:valuespace}
$\mathbf{F}$ is a three-dimensional partial value space, not a vector of
physically independent objectives: each component is an additive weighted
aggregate of normalised criteria.
\begin{sloppypar}
This distinguishes our reference set from portfolio decision analysis
approaches that compute non-dominated portfolios in a scalar value space
\citep{Liesio2007,Liesio2008,Yang2015SMAAPO}, from exact $\varepsilon$-constraint
methods for bi-objective combinatorial problems \citep{Berube2009}, and
from bi-objective mixed-integer landfill models whose objectives are
physical quantities \citep{EiseltMarianov2014}.
\end{sloppypar}
We keep the three group-level value functions unaggregated and ask how
much of the resulting trade-off surface is lost when a ranking method
collapses them into a single score before the portfolio is assembled.

\subsection{Reference set and the optimality gap}

Portfolio $t$ \emph{dominates} $t'$ when $F_1(t)\le F_1(t')$,
$F_2(t)\le F_2(t')$, and $F_3(t)\ge F_3(t')$, with at least one strict
inequality (reflecting the senses: minimise $F_1$ and $F_2$, maximise
$F_3$).
Because $|\Tset|$ is finite and enumerable, the non-dominated set
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:front}
  \Fset(w) = \bigl\{\,t\in\Tset \;:\; \nexists\,t'\in\Tset
    \text{ dominating } t\,\bigr\}
\end{equation}
is obtained \emph{exactly}, with no metaheuristic approximation.
All dominance tests use a tolerance $\varepsilon=10^{-9}$.

For dominated portfolios we report a scale-free shortfall.
With $R_\ell = \max_{t\in\Tset}F_\ell - \min_{t\in\Tset}F_\ell$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gap}
  \mathrm{gap}(t) = \max_{t'\in\Tset}\;\min\!\left(
    \frac{F_1(t){-}F_1(t')}{R_1},\;
    \frac{F_2(t){-}F_2(t')}{R_2},\;
    \frac{F_3(t'){-}F_3(t)}{R_3}
  \right)^{\!+},
\end{equation}
where $(x)^{+}=\max(0,x)$, so $\mathrm{gap}(t)=0$ if $t\in\Fset$.
The converse holds when the dominator $t'$ improves strictly on all
three objectives; if $t'$ dominates $t$ with equality on some criteria
the $\min$ operator returns zero and gap may fail to detect the
dominance (see Limitations).
The three signed differences measure by how much $t$ is dominated in
each objective in its own sense: the first two are positive when $t$
incurs higher cost or impact than $t'$, and the third is positive when
$t'$ delivers greater safety.
Normalising by the attainable range keeps the measure interpretable as a
fraction of the achievable spread;
the ideal--nadir variant is reported in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Data and preprocessing}

Stage~1 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. The dataset comprises $\Ncand$ candidate sites on Fogo Island, Cape Verde,
defined as centroids of the land-covered portion of 1~km $\times$ 1~km grid cells, retaining
only cells with at least 50\% land coverage (WGS~84 / UTM Zone~26N, grid origin at the
integer-metre bounding-box corner of the geology polygon, each point guaranteed on land
by an additional within-island validation step), each with $m=11$ criteria measured in
metres, except slope (degrees) and aspect (binary SE-facing indicator).
The spatial discretisation and criteria definition follow
\citet{AzancortNeto2026}, which provides the validated GIS dataset; the
present paper uses the same candidate set, pairwise distance matrix, and
criterion evaluations.
The criterion values used throughout are the continuous GIS measurements
in the released dataset; the discrete scoring function $\varphi(x)\to S\in\{1,2,3\}$
described in \citet{AzancortNeto2026} (their \S{}II-C and Table~3)
is not applied in the released code, and our analyses are based on the
continuous values.

The \texttt{slope\_aspect} column of the published dataset
\citep{AzancortNeto2026} is stored in a reflected convention,
$\theta_{\text{pub}} = (270^\circ - \theta) \bmod 360^\circ$, where $\theta$
is the geographic descent azimuth (0\textdegree{}=N, clockwise), computed via
the Horn algorithm (\texttt{gdaldem aspect}) on the ASTER GDEM~v3 tile.
We verified this against an independent oracle: across all $\Nvia$ admissible
sites on Fogo (admissibility filter: \texttt{dist\_protected}$>0$), the mean cosine
between the aspect and the summit-to-site bearing is $0.805$ for $\theta$ computed
from the DEM directly and $-0.102$ for $\theta_{\text{pub}}$.
The binary SE-facing indicator ($90^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ$, closed)
is invariant under this reflection, because the reflection axis
$135^\circ$ is the midpoint of the interval; the 94 SE-facing sites and
all factorial results ($Q_2$, $R$, E1, E6) are unaffected.
Only the continuous-aspect analysis (Section~\ref{sec:coding}) depends
on the convention; all values in this paper use $\theta$.
\citet{Andrade2015} assign minimum suitability to NE- and NW-facing slopes,
citing the dominant NE and NW winds and the aim of protecting urban areas
from landfill odours (p.\,254).\footnote{%
  ``Os ventos dominantes na {\'a}rea de estudo s\~ao ventos de nordeste
  e noroeste. Consequentemente, estas vertentes receberam a
  classifica\c{c}\~ao de aptid\~ao m\'inima.''~\citep[p.\,254]{Andrade2015}.}
That paper does not state the interval of maximum suitability.
The closed interval $[90^\circ, 180^\circ]$ is taken from the implementation
in \citet{AzancortNeto2026}; it is narrower than the complement of the
minimum-suitability classes, which would also include SW-facing slopes.

\paragraph{Missing values.}
Three entries are missing: roads (site~41), urban distance (site~385), and
aerodromes (site~429).
Each is imputed by the midpoint of the observed range of its criterion over
all $\Ncand$ candidates.
Because the affected sites are three out of $\Ncand$ and none enters the
exact Pareto frontier $\mathcal{F}$ under the nominal weights $\wahp$ (verified computationally), the
choice of imputation rule does not affect the min--max normalisation constants;
its effect on vector norms is negligible given the three sites account for
$3/\Ncand$ of the column.

\paragraph{Regulatory exclusion.}
Sites overlapping a protected area (\texttt{dist\_protected}$=0$) cannot
be licensed and are removed, leaving $\Nvia=\Ncand-\Nexcl$ admissible
sites.


Criterion orientation and sense follow Table~\ref{tab:criteria}.
\textit{Aspect} is the sole non-monotone criterion; its binarisation
matters for Sections~\ref{sec:three_norms} and~\ref{sec:order_ops}.

\subsection{Criterion weights}

Stage~2 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. Weights are taken from the column-normalised
priority vector of \citet{Andrade2015} (their Table~5), as adopted by
\citet{AzancortNeto2026}.
\citet{AzancortNeto2026} describe the principal eigenvector; the released weights
coincide with the column-normalised vector ($\max|\Delta|=0.001$, criterion \textit{rivers})
and differ from the eigenvector by at most $0.004$ (\textit{perm}), well below
the Dirichlet SD ($\approx0.025$ at $\kappa=200$).
Consistency is acceptable: $\lambda_{\max}=\lmax$, $\mathrm{CI}=0.08525$,
$\mathrm{RI}=1.51$ \citep{Saaty1990}, hence $\mathrm{CR}=\CR<0.10$
(threshold: \citealt{Saaty1990,Saaty2008}).
The weight vector $\wahp$ is held fixed in the base experiment and
perturbed in the Monte Carlo design of Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\subsection{Normalisation}

Stage~3 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} is the analytical focus of the paper.
Three normalisations are compared, spanning the standard taxonomies of
\citet{Jahan2015} and \citet{Aytekin2021}:
\begin{align}
  \text{min--max:} \quad &
    c_j(i) = \frac{x_{ij}-\min_j}{\max_j-\min_j}, \label{eq:mm} \\
  \text{vector:} \quad &
    c_j(i) = \frac{\tilde{x}_{ij}}{\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2},
    \quad
    \tilde{x}_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}-\min_k x_{kj}}{\max_k x_{kj}-\min_k x_{kj}},
    \label{eq:vec} \\
  \text{ranks:} \quad &
    c_j(i) = r_j(i). \label{eq:ranks}
\end{align}
Cost criteria are complemented in~\eqref{eq:mm} (subtracted from~1 after
normalisation); ranks are midranks with ties averaged.
For \textit{aspect}, the binarisation follows Table~\ref{tab:criteria};
its amplitude is $1/\sqrt{n_1}$ under vector normalisation (Section~\ref{sec:three_norms})
and $N/[2(N+1)]$ under rank normalisation (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Arank_binary}),
not the continuous formula for $\rho_j$.
The vector form in~\eqref{eq:vec} normalises the min-max column~$\tilde{x}_j$,
not the raw data.
The amplitude is therefore $A_j = \rho_j = 1/\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2$,
and the orientation (cost vs.\ benefit) is applied after normalisation via the
sign of the SAW contribution, leaving the amplitude unchanged.
Because the norm is taken after the min--max step but before cost orientation,
the amplitude of a binary or zero-inflated criterion depends on which pole is
coded as zero: coding SE-facing sites as $0$ instead of $1$ would change
$\rho_{\texttt{aspect}}$ from $\RhoAspect$ to $\RhoAspectFlipped$, and
normalising $1-\tilde{x}$ for \textit{perm} would give $\RhoPermInverted$
instead of $\RhoPerm$.
We adopt the first convention (orientation after forming the norm), consistent
with \citet{AzancortNeto2026} and the classical TOPSIS formulation in which
cost orientation is applied on the already-normalised columns
\citep{Hwang1981}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:orient_conv} lists $\rho_j$
under both conventions for all criteria.
Min--max and midranks are invariant to this choice: the amplitude is always
$1$ under min--max, and rank amplitudes depend only on the distribution of
ranks, not on which end of the scale is labelled zero.
This formulation coincides with the classical textbook form $x_{ij}/\lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_2$
when $\min_j x_j=0$ (true for \texttt{perm}, \texttt{slope}, \texttt{urban},
\texttt{cult}, \texttt{aspect} and \texttt{protected} in the 473-site dataset,
where \texttt{slope} and \texttt{protected} attain zero only at excluded candidate
sites not present in $A'$)
and differs by at most $\RhoShiftMaxPct\,\%$
(\texttt{\RhoShiftMaxCrit}) for the remaining five criteria
(\texttt{roads}, \texttt{rivers}, \texttt{coast}, \texttt{wells}, \texttt{aero}).
For SAW, switching from shift to raw norms with the same weights leaves the selected
portfolio unchanged in \RawSensSawOptSame{} of \RawSensTotal{} draws under optimal
assembly and \RawSensSawGrdSame{} under greedy; for TOPSIS the counts are
\RawSensTopsisOptSame{} and \RawSensTopsisGrdSame{}, respectively.
The two methods are thus equally insensitive to this normalisation choice,
and switching normalisations changes $Q_2$ by at most $\RawSensMaxDeltaQtwo$~pp
in any cell.
Two properties drive our results.
First, none of the three is invariant to the inclusion or exclusion of
alternatives: min--max depends on the sample extrema, vector normalisation
on the sample norm, and ranks on the sample composition.
Second, the choice interacts with the aggregation: VIKOR is defined on a
linear normalisation whereas TOPSIS is classically defined on the vector
form, a contrast noted by \citet{OpricovicTzeng2004} and implicated in
TOPSIS rank reversal \citep{GarciaCascales2012,Kuo2017}.
The reference set over which the normalisation constants are computed is
treated as an experimental factor in Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\subsection{Aggregation}

Stage~4 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. Four aggregations produce a complete ranking of the $\Nvia$ admissible
sites: SAW and TOPSIS \citep{Hwang1981}, VIKOR \citep{OpricovicTzeng2004},
and PROMETHEE~II with the usual (Type~I) preference function
\citep{Brans1986,Mareschal2008}.
In TOPSIS and VIKOR the ideal and anti-ideal points are computed on the
already oriented criterion matrix; applying the orientation a second time
inverts cost criteria; implementations that fail to separate orientation from normalisation will rank sites incorrectly.

\subsection{Portfolio assembly}

Stage~5 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. Given a site score $s(\cdot)$, two assembly rules are compared.
\emph{Greedy}: add sites in decreasing score order, skipping any site
that violates~\eqref{eq:rsep} against those already selected.
\emph{Optimal}: maximise the additive score over the feasible set,
$\argmax_{t\in\Tset}\sum_{i\in t}s(i)$, by complete enumeration
of~$\Tset$.
Both are exact given $s$; they differ only in whether the separation
constraint is handled myopically or globally.
Greedy assembly is the implicit rule whenever a ranking is read top-down,
which is the dominant practice in the applied siting literature; optimal
assembly corresponds to the ranking-plus-integer-programming paradigm
\citep{BransMareschal1992,Mavrotas2006,VetscheraDeAlmeida2012}.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

The experiment evaluates every combination of aggregation method, normalisation
rule, and assembly strategy on the Fogo Island dataset.
This section defines the three sources of weight-vector variation, the performance
measure, and the normalisation reference set used throughout.

\subsection{Three entry points for the weight vector}
\label{sec:entries}

A dominance test involves weights at three distinct places, and the
distinction is not cosmetic:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $\wsc$: the weights in the \emph{method score}, which order
    the sites and hence determine the assembled portfolio $t=M(\wsc)$;
  \item $\wob$: the weights used to map the assembled portfolio to
    objective space, $\mathbf{F}(t;\wob)$;
  \item $\wfr$: the weights used to build the reference set,
    $\Fset(\wfr)$ of~\eqref{eq:front}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Coherence condition.}
\label{rem:coherence}
A dominance test is well defined only if $\wob=\wfr$.
If they differ, the portfolio and the members of the reference set are
points of different objective spaces and their componentwise comparison
has no decision-theoretic content.
We state this explicitly because it is easy to violate silently when the
reference set is precomputed once and the Monte Carlo loop recomputes
portfolio objectives per draw.

Weights are perturbed as $w\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\kappa\,\wahp)$, $\ndraws$ draws per cell.
The primary analysis uses $\kappa=200$ (standard deviation $\approx0.025$ for $w_j=0.15$);
$\kappa=50$ ($\approx0.05$) is reported for every contrast in Table~\ref{tab:contrasts}.
$\kappa=200$ is declared in the main pre-specification document
(PRESPEC.md), committed before any factorial run;
E1 subsequently specifies the same value independently in its own
pre-specification for comparability.
Value regret ($R$) and the F8 alignment matrix were computed at $\kappa=200$ only.
$\kappa$ concentrates the draws around $\wahp$.
Throughout, $\kappa$ denotes only this concentration parameter; the
separation threshold is always written $d_{\min}=\dmin$~km.

\subsection{Performance measures and pre-specification}

The experiment evaluates each method--normalisation--assembly cell by
two performance measures.
The subscripts 2 and 3 follow a convention in which $Q_1$ would denote
a rank-based measure; we do not use $Q_1$ here.
The \emph{primary} measure is
\begin{equation}
  Q_2 = \Pr_{w}\bigl[\,M(w)\in\Fset(w)\,\bigr],
    \qquad \wsc=\wob=\wfr=w. \label{eq:Q2}
\end{equation}
$Q_2$ is the probability of reaching the frontier.
The \emph{secondary} measure is
\begin{equation}
  Q_3 = \mathbb{E}_{w}\bigl[\,\mathrm{gap}(M(w),w)\,\bigr],
    \qquad \wsc=\wob=\wfr=w, \label{eq:Q3}
\end{equation}
the expected optimality gap~\eqref{eq:gap}, which is continuous on
$[0,1]$ and retains information about how far below the frontier the
assembled portfolio falls.
$Q_3=0$ if $Q_2=1$; the converse does not hold in general: a portfolio
with $\mathrm{gap}=0$ may still be dominated (the gap measures
simultaneous improvement and can be zero for weakly dominated
portfolios). Pareto membership is determined by the dominance algorithm;
$Q_2$ is the primary measure.
Both are averaged over $\ndraws$ draws per cell.
The cell SAW/min--max/optimal satisfies $Q_2=1$ and $Q_3=0$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}; it serves as a regression test and is
excluded from substantive comparisons.

A third measure captures the loss in \emph{weighted value} rather than
frontier membership.
With $V_{\mathrm{mm}}(t;w)=\sum_{a\in t}\sum_j w_j n_j^{\mathrm{mm}}(a)$
(the SAW/min--max score under weight draw $w$),
let $t^*(w)=\argmax_t V_{\mathrm{mm}}(t;w)$ be the SAW/min--max/optimal
portfolio.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}, $t^*\in\Fset(w)$ for every $w$.
The value regret is
\begin{equation}
  R = \mathbb{E}_w\!\left[
    \frac{V_{\mathrm{mm}}(t^*;w) - V_{\mathrm{mm}}(M(w);w)}
         {V_{\mathrm{mm}}(t^*;w) - \min_{t'\in\mathcal{T}} V_{\mathrm{mm}}(t';w)}
  \right],  \label{eq:R}
\end{equation}
where $\min_{t'\in\mathcal{T}}$ is over all feasible portfolios.
$R\in[0,1]$ by construction; $R=0$ iff $M(w)=t^*$; $R=1$ iff $M(w)$ is
the worst feasible portfolio under $V_{\mathrm{mm}}$.

\paragraph{Pre-specification.}
We fixed the following before examining the factorial results; the choice
of $\kappa$ is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:entries}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
  \item \textbf{Primary outcome}: $Q_2=\Pr[M(w)\in\Fset(w)]$.
    Secondary: $Q_3=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{gap}]$.
  \item \textbf{Equivalence margin}: $\delta=2.5$~pp, defined as the
    smallest difference in $Q_2$ (or $Q_3$ scaled to pp) judged
    practically relevant for site-selection decisions.
    The aggregation contrast is declared \emph{equivalent} if the
    $90\%$ bootstrap CI lies entirely within $(-\delta,+\delta)$
    (two one-sided tests at $\alpha=0.05$ each, TOST).
  \item \textbf{Equivalence test}: TOST with $\delta=2.5$~pp tests whether the pooled mean is
    \emph{equivalent to zero} within the margin, not whether it is non-zero.
  \item \textbf{Bootstrap pairing}: replicates are paired by draw within
    each cell so that weight-vector noise cancels in contrasts; the
    within-draw correlation across cells is positive by construction
    (the same weight vector is shared), reducing contrast variance.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Normalisation reference set}

Min-max and vector constants may be computed over all $\Ncand$ candidates
(space~$A$) or over the $\Nvia$ admissible ones (space~$A'$).
Space~$A$ reproduces the source study (frontier size $|\Fset|=\FAsize$ portfolios
at the nominal AHP weights $\wahp$, reported for context only;
trios are restricted to admissible sites in both cases, with only the
normalisation reference differing between $A$ and $A'$);
space~$A'$ is the factorial design ($|\Fset|=\Fmmsize$), and the two
reference sets share only $\FAinter$ portfolios (A-space pipeline).
The source study \citep{AzancortNeto2026} applies a worst-case penalty
to the $\Nexcl$ protected-area candidates rather than excluding them
and enumerates approximately $2.90\times10^6$ feasible trios from all
$\Ncand$ candidates; restricting to the $\Nvia$ admissible sites yields
$\Ntrios$ trios and reproduces the same $\FAsize$-portfolio frontier
at the nominal weights.
Including inadmissible sites shifts the scale without those sites being
selectable, a set-dependence mechanism documented for TOPSIS by
\citet{GarciaCascales2012}.

\subsection{Factorial design}

The main experiment is a balanced $2^3$: aggregation
$\{\text{SAW},\text{TOPSIS}\}$ $\times$ normalisation
$\{\text{min--max},\text{vector}\}$ $\times$ assembly
$\{\text{greedy},\text{optimal}\}$, evaluated in $Q_2[A']$.
A wider design adds VIKOR, PROMETHEE~II and rank normalisation.
Of the $\Ncells$ nominal combinations, two are redundant because VIKOR
is invariant to per-criterion affine transformation (min--max and vector
are both affine, so VIKOR$\times$vector duplicates VIKOR$\times$min--max)
and four because PROMETHEE~II with
the usual preference function is ordinal and therefore identical under
all three normalisations, leaving $\Ncellsnet$ estimable cells;
it is unbalanced and its results are reported in the Supplementary Material.
Effects are reported in percentage points with $\nboot$-replicate
bootstrap intervals, paired by draw.
E1 uses $\nbootEone$ replicates; the factorial uses $\nboot$.
Intervals are unadjusted for multiplicity; contrasts other than the
pre-specified aggregation TOST are descriptive.

\paragraph{Benchmark circularity.}
The Pareto frontier and the objectives are both built on additive
weighted sums of min--max normalised columns, the same class of
function the factorial evaluates.
A benchmark that is internal to the value model cannot detect whether
the value model itself is mis-specified; it measures departure from
min--max rather than departure from a model-free optimum.
The normalisation contrast therefore characterises the cost of moving
away from the normalisation implicit in the benchmark, not the absolute
cost of using any particular normalisation.
The choice of min--max for the benchmark is defended within the
multi-attribute value theory (MAVT) framework on which the AHP weights
rest.
In MAVT, a criterion weight is a scale constant encoding the value of moving from the worst to the best outcome on that criterion over the declared range \citep{KeeneyRaiffa1976,BeltonStewart2002,Choo1999}.
Min--max normalisation over $\mathcal{A}'$ is the linear partial value function with this local range.
We interpret the AHP weights as MAVT scale constants anchored to $\mathcal{A}'$, which requires that the pairwise comparisons were made with the admissible range in mind.
This is a modelling assumption: AHP pairwise comparisons are not inherently anchored to attribute ranges, and the anchoring interpretation is a point of known debate \citep{BeltonStewart2002}.
Adopting a different normalisation for the benchmark objectives would
require a separate argument that the resulting frontier better represents
the decision maker's preferences; no such argument is available for
vector normalisation, which rescales each criterion by its distributional
shape rather than by the analyst's value judgement.
We therefore report the normalisation contrast as the cost of moving
away from the value model's own scale.

A separate experiment (F8) tests whether this advantage reflects
alignment between the frontier normalisation and the scoring model,
or an intrinsic property of min--max.
Section~\ref{sec:alignment} reports the design and the full cross-model
alignment results.

\section{Normalisation as implicit re-weighting: a closed-form analysis}
\label{sec:propositions}

The central analytical result of the paper is stated and derived in this section.
It takes the form of a lemma that expresses effective weights in closed form,
followed by a theorem, two propositions and one corollary that characterise the specific distortions
produced by min--max, vector, and rank normalisation.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The amplitude lemma}
\label{sec:amplitude}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

\label{sec:amp_identity}
\label{lem:amplitude}
The following states in closed form a qualitative relationship established
by \citet{Choo1999}: a weight cannot be interpreted independently of the
scale and range of its criterion.
Let $n_j : \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^N$ be a normalisation applied
column-wise to criterion $j$, and let
\begin{equation}
  A_j = \max_i n_j(x_{ij}) - \min_i n_j(x_{ij})
\end{equation}
be the \emph{amplitude} of the normalised column.
In any additive aggregation $s(i) = \sum_j w_j n_j(x_{ij})$, the
contribution of criterion $j$ to score differences between any two
alternatives is proportional to $w_j A_j$.
The effective weights are therefore
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weff}
  w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{w_j A_j}{\sum_k w_k A_k},
\end{equation}
and $w^{\mathrm{eff}} = w$ for all $w$ if and only if $A_j$ is constant
across $j$.

\textit{Derivation.}
For any two alternatives $i$ and $i'$,
$s(i) - s(i') = \sum_j w_j [n_j(x_{ij}) - n_j(x_{i'j})]$.
The maximum absolute contribution of criterion $j$ to this difference,
over all pairs $(i,i')$, is $w_j A_j$.
The amplitude-weighted contribution of criterion $j$, normalised across
criteria, is therefore $w_j A_j / \sum_k w_k A_k$, which is $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}$
in~\eqref{eq:weff}.
The ``if and only if'' follows because $w^{\mathrm{eff}} = w$ iff
$w_j A_j / \sum_k w_k A_k = w_j$ for all $j$, which holds iff all
$A_j$ equal the same constant.\hfill$\square$

For affine normalisation $n_j(x) = b_j + A_j \tilde{n}_j(x)$ (which
includes min--max and vector), the score reduces to
$s(i) = \mathrm{const} + \sum_j w_j A_j \tilde{n}_j(x_{ij})$, identical
to SAW with weights $w^{\mathrm{eff}}$ applied to the column $\tilde{n}_j$.
The ranking under any affine normalisation is therefore equivalent to SAW
under min--max with weights $w^{\mathrm{eff}}$.

\paragraph{Scope and empirical confirmation.}
\label{rem:scope}
The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} is exact for additive aggregation
under affine normalisation (including min--max and vector);
for rank normalisation, midranks are not affine in the original values,
so the effective weight $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}$ approximates the weight shift
rather than determining it exactly.
Within this scope, $Q_2[\mathcal{A}']$ at $\kappa=200$ is $\QtwommOptimal\%$,
$\QtwoRanksOptimal\%$ (heuristic: ranks fall outside the exact scope) and
$\QtwovecOptimal\%$ for min--max, ranks and vector normalisation
respectively; the distortions are $\mathrm{TV}=0$, $0.091$ and $\TVvec$,
a strictly monotone ordering on this instance (Table~\ref{tab:tvorder}).

TOPSIS and VIKOR aggregate distances to ideal points rather than
weighted sums, so amplitudes enter quadratically or in ratio form and
the closed form does not transfer.
Two consequences are observable.
First, VIKOR is invariant to per-criterion affine transformation,
because both scale and shift cancel in its ratio form; min--max and
vector normalisation are both affine, so the VIKOR$\times$vector cell
is identical to VIKOR$\times$min--max and is excluded from the design.
Second, by analogy with the quadratic exponent, TOPSIS amplifies the
criterion contribution non-linearly, so min--max is not amplitude-neutral for it
(Remark~\ref{rem:mm_not_neutral}).

\subsection{Amplitude under the three standard normalisations}
\label{sec:three_norms}

A natural measure of aggregate distortion is the total variation
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:TV}
  \mathrm{TV} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_j |w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} - w_j|,
\end{equation}
which is invariant to multiplication of all $A_j$ by a common constant,
since that constant cancels in the renormalisation of~\eqref{eq:weff}.
Any faithful measure of re-weighting must inherit this invariance;
$D = \sum_j w_j |A_j - 1|$ does not, and is therefore not an
appropriate summary of the distortion.
The amplitude takes the following form in each of the three families.

\textbf{1) Min--max.}\;
$A_j \equiv 1$ by construction; hence $w^{\mathrm{eff}} = w$.
Min--max is the unique member of the three that preserves elicited
weights exactly under additive aggregation.

\textbf{2) Vector.}\;
$A_j = \rho_j = 1/\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2$,
where $\tilde{x}_j(a)=\bigl(x_j(a)-\min_j x_j\bigr)/\bigl(\max_j x_j-\min_j x_j\bigr)$;
the range of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j$ is always~$1$, so $A_j = 1/\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2$
regardless of the raw scale.
For the binary criterion ($\tilde{x}_j = x_j \in\{0,1\}$) this reduces to
$A_j = 1/\sqrt{n_1}$, since $\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2=\sqrt{n_1}$
for a binary min--max column.
Criteria whose column minimum is tied by many sites obtain large $\rho_j$
(\textit{perm}: 350 zeros compresses $\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2$);
criteria with mass spread over the range obtain small $\rho_j$.

\textbf{3) Midranks scaled by $N+1$.}\;
$A_j = (r_j^{\max} - r_j^{\min})/(N+1)$, which approaches~$1$
when ties are absent but contracts with tie mass.
For a binary criterion with $n_0$ zeros and $n_1 = N-n_0$ ones,
the midranks of the two groups are $(1+n_0)/2$ and $(n_0+1+N)/2$,
giving
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Arank_binary}
  A_j = \frac{N}{2(N+1)} \approx \frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
The result is \emph{independent of $n_1$}: every binary criterion
loses approximately half its nominal weight in rank space, whatever the
class balance.
A binary indicator contributes half the amplitude of a continuous
criterion with no ties.\hfill$\square$

\noindent\textbf{Empirical calibration.}
Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} reports $A_j$ for the three normalisations on
the $\Nvia$ Fogo Island sites, along with the total variation
$\mathrm{TV} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_j |w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} - w_j|$ as a
summary statistic of aggregate distortion.
Min--max gives $\mathrm{TV} = 0$ by construction.
The vector normalisation gives $\mathrm{TV} = \TVvec$: criterion
\textit{perm} dominates, with $\rho_j = 0.583$ (factor
$\mathbf{\PermInflation\times}$ relative to nominal weight) because its raw
distribution has 350 exact zeros and a maximum of~532~m, giving a
long-tailed norm.
The rank normalisation gives $\mathrm{TV} = 0.091$: \textit{perm} is
deflated ($A_j = 0.547$, factor $\mathbf{0.616\times}$) because 350
tied zeros compress the maximum midrank to $\approx 214.5$ instead
of~$389$, while \textit{aspect} (binary) gives $A_j = N/[2(N+1)]
\approx 0.499$ (factor $\mathbf{0.562\times}$) as predicted by
\eqref{eq:Arank_binary}, independent of $n_1$.
The seven fully continuous criteria are conversely \emph{inflated}
(factor $\approx 1.12\times$) because the mean amplitude $\bar{A}<1$
makes each criterion's unit relative weight larger; \textit{perm} and
\textit{aspect} lose weight, while \textit{urban} and \textit{cult} gain
slightly less than $\approx\RnkContinuousGainPct\%$ (see Table~\ref{tab:amplitude}).
The directions are reversed: the same heavy-zero distribution inflates
\textit{perm} under vector normalisation and deflates it under rank
scaling.
Figure~\ref{fig:binary_amplitude} illustrates the binary case analytically.

\subsection{Pareto consistency of optimal linear aggregation}
\label{prop:p1}

\begin{proposition}[Pareto consistency of optimal linear aggregation]
\label{prop:p1:stmt}
Let $s(a)=\sum_{j\in G_1\cup G_2} w_j\bigl(1-c_j(a)\bigr)
          +\sum_{j\in G_3} w_j c_j(a)$
be the SAW score on the oriented decision matrix, built with the same
weights and normalisation as \eqref{eq:F1}--\eqref{eq:F3}, with
$w_j>0$ for all $j$.
Then $T^{*}=\argmax_{T\in\Tset}\sum_{a\in T}s(a)$ satisfies
$T^{*}\in\Fset$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Because $|T|=\kk$ is fixed, summing $s$ over $T$ gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:scalarisation}
  \sum_{a\in T}s(a) &\;=\; \kk\,W_{G_1\cup G_2}
    \;-\;F_1(T)\;-\;F_2(T)\;+\;F_3(T), \notag\\
  &\quad W_{G_1\cup G_2}=\sum_{j\in G_1\cup G_2} w_j,
\end{align}
so maximising $\sum_{a\in T}s(a)$ is equivalent to minimising
$F_1(T)+F_2(T)-F_3(T)$, a strictly positive scalarisation of the three
objectives in their own senses (minimise $F_1$ and $F_2$, maximise $F_3$).
Suppose $T'$ dominated $T^{*}$, that is $F_1(T')\le F_1(T^{*})$,
$F_2(T')\le F_2(T^{*})$, $F_3(T')\ge F_3(T^{*})$ with at least one
strict inequality.
Then $F_1(T')+F_2(T')-F_3(T') < F_1(T^{*})+F_2(T^{*})-F_3(T^{*})$,
whence $\sum_{a\in T'}s(a) > \sum_{a\in T^{*}}s(a)$, contradicting the
optimality of $T^{*}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The $Q_2=1$ cell is a regression test, not evidence]
\label{rem:definitional}
Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt} holds for \emph{any} normalisation,
provided the normalisation used to compute $F_\ell$ matches the one in
$s$.
The $Q_2=1$ cell therefore holds by construction and carries no
evidential weight about min--max in particular; we use it solely as a
regression test, and any implementation returning $Q_2<1$ there is
faulty.
Its informative content is the \emph{contrapositive}: every cell falling
below $100\%$ does so because some premise fails (group-constant amplitudes
relative to the objectives (Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}), additivity, or
global assembly), and the factorial
identifies which.
\end{remark}

\noindent\textbf{Empirical verification.}
SAW with min--max normalisation over the $\Nvia$ viable sites:
$Q_2[\mathcal{A}'] = 100.0\%$ for both $\kappa \in \{50, 200\}$
($\ndraws$ draws each).

Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt} handles $\mathrm{TV}=0$; when $\mathrm{TV}>0$, the total variation~\eqref{eq:TV} is the $\ell^1$ norm of the deviation
vector $\delta_j=w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}-w_j$, and is therefore blind to how
that deviation is spread across criteria.
Two distortions of identical $\mathrm{TV}$, one concentrated on a
single heavily weighted criterion and one spread over several, reorder
the alternatives very differently, because deviations of opposite sign
partially cancel in the weighted sum when they are distributed.
The following identity separates the two dimensions.

\begin{proposition}[Magnitude--concentration factorisation]
\label{prop:concentration}
Let $\delta_j = w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} - w_j$ be the deviation induced by a
normalisation, so that $\sum_j \delta_j = 0$ and
$\sum_j |\delta_j| = 2\,\mathrm{TV}$ by~\eqref{eq:TV}.
Define the \emph{effective number of distorted criteria} as the
participation ratio
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:meff}
  m_{\mathrm{eff}}
    = \frac{\bigl(\sum_j |\delta_j|\bigr)^2}{\sum_j \delta_j^2}
    = \frac{\lVert\delta\rVert_1^2}{\lVert\delta\rVert_2^2}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:l2fact}
  \lVert\delta\rVert_2
    = \frac{2\,\mathrm{TV}}{\sqrt{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}},
\end{equation}
and $m_{\mathrm{eff}} \ge 2$ (lower bound) and $m_{\mathrm{eff}} \le m$ (upper bound).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:l2fact} is immediate from~\eqref{eq:meff}:
$\lVert\delta\rVert_2 = \lVert\delta\rVert_1/\sqrt{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}$
and $\lVert\delta\rVert_1 = 2\,\mathrm{TV}$.
For the lower bound, let $S=\sum_{\delta_j>0}\delta_j$.
Since $\sum_j\delta_j=0$ we have $S=-\sum_{\delta_j<0}\delta_j>0$,
so $\lVert\delta\rVert_1=2S$.
Also $\lVert\delta\rVert_2^2\le 2S^2$
(because the positive and negative parts each contribute at most $S^2$
to the squared norm, with equality when each part is concentrated on a
single component).
Therefore $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=\lVert\delta\rVert_1^2/\lVert\delta\rVert_2^2\ge 4S^2/(2S^2)=2$.
The upper bound is Cauchy--Schwarz: $\lVert\delta\rVert_1^2\le m\,\lVert\delta\rVert_2^2$,
so $m_{\mathrm{eff}}\le m$.
The upper bound is attained when all $|\delta_j|$ coincide; the lower bound
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}=2$ is attained for $\delta=(c,-c,0,\dots,0)$ with $c\neq0$
(requires $m\ge2$ and an even-symmetric pattern).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the factorisation buys]
\label{rem:meff_reading}
$\mathrm{TV}$ answers \emph{how much} the elicited weights were moved;
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}$ answers \emph{over how many criteria}, on a scale
whose endpoints are fixed by $m$ alone and therefore comparable across
instances.
Neither is redundant: the Euclidean norm~\eqref{eq:l2fact} is their
ratio, and in a synthetic grid reported in the Supplementary Material it
tracks efficiency loss better than $\mathrm{TV}$ alone.
Both are computable from the normalised decision matrix in one line, and
we recommend reporting the pair $(\mathrm{TV},\,m_{\mathrm{eff}})$
rather than $\mathrm{TV}$ alone (Section~\ref{sec:practice}).
\end{remark}

For Fogo Island, computing~\eqref{eq:meff} on the deviations implied by
Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} under vector normalisation gives
$\mathrm{TV}=\TVvec$, $\lVert\delta\rVert_2=\DeltaLtwoshift$,
$\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty=\DeltaLinfshift$ (attained at \textit{perm}), and
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}=\meff$ on the admissible range $[2,\,11]$.
The distortion is thus not merely large but \emph{concentrated}: a
single criterion accounts for $\PermAbsDevPct\%$ of the total absolute deviation,
replacing the qualitative observation that \textit{perm} is an
adversarial column with a position on a bounded scale.

\subsection{Which part of the distortion costs efficiency}
\label{sec:groupconst}

Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt} requires the score and the objectives to
share a normalisation.
The next result relaxes this to a condition on how amplitudes vary
across criteria.

\begin{theorem}[When exact assembly survives normalisation]
\label{thm:groupconst}
Fix weights $w>0$ and a score whose normalised columns are strictly
increasing affine functions of the oriented min--max partial values,
$n_j=b_j+A_j\,\tilde c_j$ with $A_j>0$, where $\tilde c_j=1-c_j$ for
$j\in G_1\cup G_2$ and $\tilde c_j=c_j$ for $j\in G_3$, and let the
objectives~\eqref{eq:F1}--\eqref{eq:F3} be built on min--max with the
same weights $w>0$.
The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
  \item For every set of sites and every feasible family $\Tset$ of
    portfolios of size $\kk$,
    $T^{*}=\argmax_{T\in\Tset}\sum_{a\in T}s(a)$ belongs to $\Fset(w)$.
  \item The amplitudes are constant within each objective group:
    $A_j=\bar A_g$ for all $j\in G_g$, $g=1,2,3$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{(ii)$\Rightarrow$(i).}
Summing $s(a)=\sum_j w_j n_j(a)$ over a portfolio of fixed size $\kk$
gives
$\sum_{a\in T}s(a)=C-\bar A_1F_1(T)-\bar A_2F_2(T)+\bar A_3F_3(T)$,
where $C$ does not depend on $T$.
Maximising the score is therefore a strictly positive scalarisation of
the three objectives in their own senses, and the argument of
Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt} applies unchanged; this is the classical
result that maximisers of a strictly positive weighted sum are (properly)
efficient \citep{Geoffrion1968}.

\textbf{(i)$\Rightarrow$(ii).}
Suppose $j,k\in G_g$ with $A_j<A_k$.
Consider an instance containing, besides sites $a$ and $a'$, sites that
attain the minimum and maximum of every criterion, so that the min--max
ranges, and hence $A$, are fixed independently of $a$ and $a'$.
Let $a$ and $a'$ coincide on every criterion except $j$ and $k$, and let
$T=\{a\}\cup R$ and $T'=\{a'\}\cup R$ for a common set $R$ of
$(\kk-1)=2$ other sites.
Assign oriented differences
$w_j[\tilde c_j(a')-\tilde c_j(a)]=x$ and
$w_k[\tilde c_k(a')-\tilde c_k(a)]=y$, where $y=-\eta<0$ and
$x=\tfrac{\eta}{2}\,(1+A_k/A_j)$, with $\eta>0$ small enough to keep all
partial values in $[0,1]$.
Then $x+y=\tfrac{\eta}{2}(A_k/A_j-1)>0$, so the portfolio $T'$ that
contains $a'$ improves objective $g$ over the portfolio $T$ that
contains $a$ instead, and leaves the other two objectives unchanged:
$T'$ dominates $T$.
Yet $A_jx+A_ky=\tfrac{\eta}{2}(A_j-A_k)<0$, so $T$ has the strictly
higher score.
On the feasible family $\Tset=\{T,T'\}$ exact assembly returns the
dominated portfolio, contradicting~(i).
\end{proof}

The theorem treats the affine score map as given.
For data-dependent normalisations such as vector normalisation the
amplitudes are themselves computed on the instance, so~(i)$\Rightarrow$(ii)
should be read as: whenever a normalisation produces amplitudes that vary
within a group, there exist configurations of sites under which exact
assembly selects a dominated portfolio.
Section~\ref{sec:e1} shows that Fogo is such a configuration.

Vector normalisation has this affine form with $A_j=\rho_j$, so the
theorem applies to it whenever $\rho_j$ is constant within groups.
The condition suggests characterising the deviation
$\delta=w^{\mathrm{eff}}-w$ by two complementary indices.
With group totals $W_g=\sum_{j\in G_g}w_j$ and
$W_g^{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{j\in G_g}w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}$, the
\emph{between-group} distortion is
$\mathrm{TV}_B=\tfrac12\sum_g|W_g^{\mathrm{eff}}-W_g|$, and the
\emph{within-group} distortion is
$\mathrm{TV}_W=\sum_g W_g\,\mathrm{TV}_{W,g}$ with
$\mathrm{TV}_{W,g}=\tfrac12\sum_{j\in G_g}
|w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}/W_g^{\mathrm{eff}}-w_j/W_g|$.
These indices are not an additive partition of
$\tfrac12\sum_j|w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}-w_j|$: the group-level effective
weight satisfies $W_g^{\mathrm{eff}}=W_g\bar{A}_g/S$, where
$\bar{A}_g=\sum_{j\in G_g}(w_j/W_g)A_j$ and $S=\sum_j w_j A_j$;
$\mathrm{TV}_B$ captures the full distortion only when $\mathrm{TV}_W=0$.
Under group-constant amplitudes $\mathrm{TV}_W=0$, whatever the value of
$\mathrm{TV}_B$.
The theorem therefore predicts that between-group distortion alone
relocates the optimum along the frontier without leaving it.
Section~\ref{sec:e1} tests this prediction on Fogo.
Using the amplitudes of Table~\ref{tab:amplitude}, vector normalisation
gives $\mathrm{TV}_B=\TVBvec$ and $\mathrm{TV}_W=\TVWvec$.

\subsection{The amplitude exponent: extension beyond additive aggregation}
\label{sec:exponent}

The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} is exact for SAW ($\alpha=1$).
For distance-based aggregation, treating the squared Euclidean distance in
TOPSIS as homogeneous of degree $\alpha=2$ gives
$w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}(\alpha)\propto w_j^\alpha A_j^\alpha$;
VIKOR is invariant to per-criterion affine transformations and is
unaffected by normalisation; PROMETHEE~II with the usual criterion is
ordinal-invariant (Section~\ref{prop:p2}).
Full derivations and Table~S1 (TV by normalisation and exponent) are in
the Supplementary Material.

\begin{remark}[Amplitude structure beyond additive aggregation]
\label{prop:exponent}
The exponent analogy ($\alpha=2$ for TOPSIS) implies that
$\mathrm{TV}(2)-\mathrm{TV}(1)>0$ whenever amplitudes are non-constant.
This does not imply a monotone relationship between
$\mathrm{TV}(2)-\mathrm{TV}(1)$ and $Q_2$ loss: the analogy holds
for the squared distance, not for the proximity index
$C_i=d_i^-/(d_i^-+d_i^+)$, which is not linear.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Min--max is not amplitude-neutral for TOPSIS]
\label{rem:mm_not_neutral}
Under $\alpha=2$ and $A_j\equiv1$ (min--max),
$w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}\propto w_j^{2}$, giving $\mathrm{TV}(2)=0.160$
on the Fogo weights.
Combining min--max with TOPSIS therefore re-weights criteria through
the quadratic exponent; the effect on $C_i$ is not a simple linear
re-weighting.
\end{remark}

% ---

\subsection{PROMETHEE~II with usual criterion equals rank-weighted SAW}
\label{prop:p2}
Let $P_j(d) = \mathbf{1}[d > 0]$ (the usual criterion).
With $r_j(a)$ the midrank of site $a$ on criterion $j$, the
PROMETHEE~II net flow satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p2identity}
  \phi(a) = \frac{2}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^m w_j\, r_j(a) - \frac{N+1}{N-1}
  \quad (\textstyle\sum_j w_j = 1),
\end{equation}
for any tied-value pattern; the ranking induced by $\phi$ is therefore
identical to that of $\tilde{s}(a) = \sum_j w_j r_j(a)$.
The derivation (via midrank counting), the rank-scaling caveat for
heavy-zero criteria, and the empirical verification on Fogo
($6.7\times10^{-16}$ maximum absolute error) are in the Supplementary
Material.

\begin{remark}[Rank-space amplitude and TV invariance]
\label{rem:p2normalization}
Dividing midranks by $N+1$ is a common scale factor; TV is invariant to
common rescaling (Eq.~\eqref{eq:TV}).
The distortion arises from the tie structure of individual criteria,
not from the rescaling constant: the $350$ tied zeros in \texttt{perm}
compress its column to $[0.003,\,0.550]$ while every tie-free continuous criterion
spans $[0.003,\,0.997]$, deflating \texttt{perm}'s effective weight by
$\approx 0.616\times$, the rank-space counterpart of its
$\PermInflation\times$ inflation under vector normalisation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Vector normalisation distorts effective weights}
\label{prop:p3}
Under vector normalisation, $v_j(a) = \tilde{x}_j(a) / \lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2$.
The amplitude is $A_j = \rho_j$ as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:amplitude}, and by~\eqref{eq:weff}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weff_vec}
  w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{w_j\,\rho_j}{\sum_k w_k\,\rho_k},
  \qquad
  \rho_j = \frac{\max_a \tilde{x}_j(a) - \min_a \tilde{x}_j(a)}
                {\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2}.
\end{equation}
For binary criteria, $\rho_j = 1/\sqrt{n_1}$ exactly, where $n_1$ is
the number of ones.

\textit{Derivation.}
The range of $v_j(a)$ over all sites is
$(\max_a \tilde{x}_j(a) - \min_a \tilde{x}_j(a)) /
  \lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2
  = 1/\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2 = \rho_j$,
so $A_j = \rho_j$ and~\eqref{eq:weff_vec} follows from~\eqref{eq:weff}.
\begin{sloppypar}
For binary $\tilde{x}_j \in \{0,1\}$ (min-max of binary is binary)
with $n_1$ ones,
$\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_j\rVert_2 = \sqrt{n_1}$ and
$\max - \min = 1$, giving $\rho_j = 1/\sqrt{n_1}$.\hfill$\square$
\end{sloppypar}

\noindent\textbf{Empirical calibration.}
Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} lists $\rho_j$ and $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}/w_j$
for all eleven criteria.
\textit{Perm} ($w_j = 0.152$): $\rho_j = 0.583$, effective-weight
factor $\mathbf{\PermInflation\times}$.
\textit{Urban} ($w_j = 0.166$): factor $\mathbf{\UrbanRatioVec\times}$.
\textit{Aspect} (binary, $n_1 = 94$): $\rho_j = 1/\sqrt{94} = 0.103$,
factor $\mathbf{\AspectRatioVec\times}$.

\subsection{Order of operations under vector normalisation}
\label{sec:order_ops}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

\begin{corollary}[Order of operations under vector normalisation]
\label{cor:order}
For a criterion complemented to a cost orientation, the amplitude
$A_j^{\rm vec}$ depends on whether the complement precedes or follows
normalisation.
Min--max is affine, so $A_j^{\rm mm}$ is invariant to the order.
Vector normalisation is not: complementing first normalises
$\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{x}$ and complementing afterwards normalises
$\mathbf{x}$.
For a binary column with $n_1$ ones and $n_0=N-n_1$ zeros:
\[
  A_j^{\rm comp\text{-}first} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_0}},
  \quad
  A_j^{\rm norm\text{-}first} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_1}},
  \quad
  \text{ratio} = \sqrt{n_0/n_1}.
\]
The two normalised columns are not collinear (they are linearly
independent, since neither is a scalar multiple
of the other), and each has a different amplitude $A_j$.
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Empirical magnitude on Fogo Island.}
For \textit{aspect} ($n_1=94$ SE-facing sites, $n_0=295$), the two orders
give $A_j\in\{0.058,\,0.103\}$, a factor of $\AspectAmplitudeRatio$.
Running TOPSIS with vector normalisation under each order, holding the
criterion semantics (SE-facing $=$ favourable), weights, and data fixed,
yields a Kendall~$\tau_b=\AspectTauB$ and top-100 set agreement of $\AspectTopKAgree$
across the $\Nvia$ admissible sites.
The agreement is high because \textit{aspect} is one of eleven criteria:
the $\AspectAmplitudeRatio\times$ amplitude difference in one binary column is partly
absorbed by the other ten.
The divergence between the two orders would widen in proportion to
$\sqrt{n_0/n_1}$ and would be undetectable in any output that a
practitioner routinely reports.

\subsection{Consequences for the factorial comparison}
\label{sec:consequences}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} and
Sections~\ref{prop:p1}--\ref{prop:p3} partition the sources of
sub-optimality in the factorial experiment into four ordered factors:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Normalisation effect (min--max vs.\ others):}
    Min--max is the only normalisation that preserves elicited weights
    in additive aggregations (Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}).
    Under SAW, where the Lemma applies exactly, both the greedy and
    optimal assembly benefit from the preservation.
  \item \emph{Assembly effect (optimal vs.\ greedy):}
    Exact assembly helps under min--max and, on frontier attainment ($Q_2$), hurts under vector
    normalisation; on value regret ($R$), exact assembly achieves lower regret than greedy
    under both normalisations: for TOPSIS/min--max the difference is $\RegrDiffTOPSISmmGrdOpt$~pp
    ($[\RegrDiffTOPSISmmGrdOptLo,\,\RegrDiffTOPSISmmGrdOptHi]$~pp);
    for SAW/min--max $R=0$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt} and carries no evidential weight
    (Section~\ref{sec:assembly}).
  \item \emph{Aggregation effect (SAW vs.\ TOPSIS vs.\ VIKOR):}
    TOPSIS and VIKOR aggregate through ideal-point distances;
    The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} does not apply exactly to them.
    The factorial experiment measures the residual effect after
    controlling for items~1 and~2.
  \item \emph{Reference-set effect (normalisation over $A$ vs.\ $A'$):}
    Including inadmissible sites in the normalisation reference shifts
    the scale without those sites being selectable
    (Section~\ref{sec:alignment}, Table~\ref{tab:coding2x2}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Empirical ordering by TV}
\label{sec:lemmaconf}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} characterises the weight deviation
the normalisation imposes on the elicited weights, measured
on the simplex by~\eqref{eq:TV} with $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}$ from~\eqref{eq:weff}.
Total variation is the appropriate measure because, as noted after
Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}, $w^{\mathrm{eff}}$ is invariant to a common
rescaling of all amplitudes: a factor $c>0$ applied to every $A_j$
cancels in~\eqref{eq:TV} and produces no re-weighting.

Table~\ref{tab:tvorder} pairs the distortion with observed
performance on Fogo, restricted to additive aggregation, the scope in which
the lemma is exact.
The three normalisations display a monotone ordering on this instance;
the amplitude identity alone does not imply a monotone relationship
between $\mathrm{TV}$ and Pareto attainment in general.

\subsection{Factorial results}
\label{sec:factorial}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

Table~\ref{tab:factorial} reports the balanced $2^3$ design and
Table~\ref{tab:contrasts} the contrasts, with $\nboot$-replicate bootstrap
intervals paired by draw.

\textbf{Severity.}
The secondary measure $Q_3$ separates the min--max cells from the vector
cells as $Q_2$ does: it is below $0.2\%$ of the attainable range in
every min--max cell and between $0.86\%$ and $1.14\%$ in every vector cell.
Within the vector cells the two measures order the cells differently,
because $Q_3$ combines how often and how far a cell misses the frontier.
A portfolio that misses the frontier falls on average $1$--$3\%$ of the
attainable range below it (Table~\ref{tab:factorial}, conditional gap column).
Under vector normalisation, exact assembly misses the frontier more often
than greedy assembly but by a smaller margin (conditional gap
$\CondGapSAWvecOpt\%$ against $\CondGapSAWvecGrd\%$ for SAW;
$\CondGapTOPSISvecOpt\%$ against $\CondGapTOPSISvecGrd\%$ for TOPSIS).
This is consistent with exact assembly reaching the frontier of
$w^{\mathrm{eff}}$, which lies close to, but not on, the frontier of $w$.
According to the pre-specified equivalence criterion ($\delta=2.5$~pp),
all differences in $Q_3$ scaled to percentage points are below $\delta$;
severity differences are not practically relevant on this instance.

\textbf{Value regret.}
The regret $R$~\eqref{eq:R} shows that $Q_2$ and $R$ measure distinct
aspects of performance (Table~\ref{tab:factorial}, $R$ column).
Under min--max normalisation, TOPSIS/mm/optimal reaches the frontier on
$\QtwoTOPSISmmOpt\%$ of draws ($Q_2$) yet loses $\RegrTOPSISmmOpt$~pp
of the value range ($R$): a portfolio that is almost always
Pareto-efficient is not the value-maximising frontier portfolio.
By construction, $R=0$ only for SAW/min--max/optimal.
The gap between $Q_2$ and $R$ is largest for TOPSIS under both
normalisations and for SAW under vector normalisation, confirming that
scoring distortion shifts which frontier portfolio is selected even when
it does not push the portfolio off the frontier entirely.
Under vector normalisation the assembly effect also reverses: exact assembly
reaches the frontier less often than greedy
($Q_2 = \QtwovecOptimal\%$ against $\QtwovecGreedy\%$; SAW)
but achieves lower regret ($R = \RegrSAWvecOpt$ against $\RegrSAWvecGrd$~pp,
difference $\RegrDiffSAWvec$~pp, 95\%~CI
$[\RegrDiffSAWvecLo,\,\RegrDiffSAWvecHi]$~pp; below the $\delta=2.5$~pp
pre-specified margin for $Q_2$ contrasts, applied here by analogy).
The portfolios selected by exact optimisation of the distorted score,
though they miss the frontier of~$w$ more often, are better portfolios
under the elicited weights~$w$.
For context, the median regret of a randomly drawn valid trio on this
instance is approximately $\RRegretRandom$~pp; all eight cells are far
into the upper tail of the portfolio-quality distribution.

\paragraph{Why pooling aggregation families obscures the mechanism.}
\label{rem:pooling}
Averaging $Q_2$ across all aggregation families mixes methods that
inherit the amplitude distortion with one that is immune to it by
construction.
VIKOR is invariant to per-criterion affine transformations (scale and
shift both cancel in the ratio form); min--max and vector normalisation
are both per-criterion affine, so the VIKOR$\times$vector cell
duplicates VIKOR$\times$min--max, and pooling it inflates the vector
average.
Rank normalisation under distance-based aggregation degrades performance
far more than the additive re-weighting accounts for, because ranks
distort the Euclidean geometry on which those methods depend.
Both effects are outside the scope of the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} and
both are predicted by it as exclusions.
We therefore report the additive cells as the test of the mechanism and
the full factorial as a description of practice.

\subsection{Exact assembly: an effect conditional on normalisation}
\label{sec:assembly}

Tables~\ref{tab:factorial} and~\ref{tab:contrasts} show the per-cell $Q_2$ values and contrasts. We do not report a contrast pooled over all cells: the effect changes
sign with the normalisation, and any average would describe neither regime.
Pooled over the three informative cells (excluding SAW/min--max/optimal, fixed
at $100\%$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}), it is $\AsmThreecell$~pp
($95\%$ CI $[\AsmThreecellLo,\,\AsmThreecellHi]$); including that cell, it is $-1.1$~pp. The
difference between the two figures is itself an artefact of the constructed cell.

Under min--max normalisation, exact assembly is guaranteed to reach the
frontier for SAW (Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}); the $+14.0$~pp contrast in
that cell measures the greedy shortfall (Section~\ref{sec:greedy}), not an
empirical benefit of optimisation. For TOPSIS, whose score is not additive in
the objectives, exact assembly adds $+\AsmMMtopsis$~pp
($95\%$ CI $[+\AsmMMtopsisLo,\,+\AsmMMtopsisHi]$), a gain whose lower bound
is close to zero. Under vector normalisation, exact assembly is worse than
reading the ranking top-down at $\kappa=200$ for both aggregations: $-\AsmVecSAW$~pp for SAW
($[-\AsmVecSAWLo,\,-\AsmVecSAWHi]$) and $-\AsmVecTOPSIS$~pp for TOPSIS
($[-\AsmVecTOPSISLo,\,-\AsmVecTOPSISHi]$). For
TOPSIS the assembly-by-normalisation interaction is $+\InteractionTOPSIS$~pp ($95\%$ CI
$[+\InteractionTOPSISLo,\,+\InteractionTOPSISHi]$).

The reversal has a direct reading. Optimal assembly maximises
$\sum_{i\in t} s(i)$ exactly and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}, reaches
the frontier defined by the weights the score encodes. Under vector
normalisation those weights are $w^{\mathrm{eff}}$, which raise
\textit{perm}'s share of $G_1$ by $\approx\IntraGperm\times$ (and its overall
weight by $\approx \PermInflation\times$); exact assembly therefore pursues the trade-offs
of $w^{\mathrm{eff}}$ precisely, and departs from the frontier defined by $w$.
Greedy assembly is myopic and, on this evidence, less exposed to a distortion
it does not optimise. This last statement is an interpretation;
Section~\ref{sec:e1} isolates the component of the distortion responsible.
Value regret ($R$, Table~\ref{tab:factorial}) corroborates it:
under vector normalisation exact assembly achieves $\RegrSAWvecOpt$~pp regret
against $\RegrSAWvecGrd$~pp for greedy ($\RegrDiffSAWvec$~pp less,
95\%~CI $[\RegrDiffSAWvecLo,\,\RegrDiffSAWvecHi]$~pp;
below the $\delta=2.5$~pp practical-significance margin, applied here by analogy).
The portfolios selected by exact optimisation of the distorted score,
though they miss the frontier of~$w$ more often, are better portfolios
under the elicited weights~$w$ (Section~\ref{sec:factorial}).

\subsection{Coding inconsistency: a failure mode outside the theorem}
\label{sec:coding}\label{rem:nonmonotone}
Scoring \textit{aspect} as a continuous geographic azimuth while the
objectives use a binary SE-facing indicator ($90^\circ$--$180^\circ$,
closed) introduces a non-monotone mismatch.
The score column is the min--max normalisation of the geographic azimuth
$\theta\in[0^\circ,360^\circ)$: the site with the highest azimuth
receives score~$1$; scores decrease monotonically as $\theta$ decreases,
reaching~$0$ at the minimum azimuth (NNE, near $0^\circ$).
The discontinuity is at due north: a site at $359^\circ$ scores near~$1$
while a site at $1^\circ$ scores near~$0$, even though both face nearly
the same direction.
SE-facing sites ($\theta\in[90^\circ,180^\circ]$) lie in the lower-middle
range of the scale and receive mid-range scores; ordering sites by azimuth
places the top quartile at WNW-facing azimuths (circular mean
$\approx302^\circ$, s.d.\ $27^\circ$) by construction of the linear
scale, not as an empirical finding; those azimuths belong to the class
that \citet{Andrade2015} classify as minimum suitability on Fogo.
Because the SE-facing indicator is a deterministic function of $\theta$
($b_\mathrm{asp}=\mathbf{1}[\theta\in[90^\circ,180^\circ]]$), the
Spearman correlation between normalised azimuth and the indicator
($r_s=\AspSpearman$, point-biserial on ranks, descriptive) measures
only where the SE class falls on the linear scale, not an empirical
association.
This coding mismatch reduces $Q_2$ from $100\%$ to $\RaspOptimal\%$ under
SAW/min--max/optimal: $\RaspLoss$~pp through coding alone
(Table~\ref{tab:coding2x2}).
With a consistent encoding, normalising over the $\Ncand$-site set costs
$0$~pp and improves greedy by $\RefSetGreedyGain$~pp
($95\%$~CI $[\RoneTwoGrdCIlo,\,\RoneTwoGrdCIhi]$).
A non-monotone recoding falls outside Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}, which
requires the score column to be an affine function of the evaluator's
partial value; this is therefore a distinct failure mode, not the
amplitude mechanism.
Full details and provenance are in the Supplementary Material.


\subsection{The greedy shortfall}
\label{sec:greedy}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

Section~\ref{prop:p1} guarantees that optimal assembly of an
additive score attains the reference set under matching weights;
Table~\ref{tab:factorial} shows greedy assembly of the same score
reaching only $86.0\%$.
The shortfall is attributable to the assembly rule alone, with
aggregation, normalisation and reference set held fixed.

Draw~$302$ ($\kappa=200$) exhibits it concretely.
The greedy portfolio is $\{184,279,320\}$ with $\mathrm{gap}=0.028$:
it is dominated by an attainable portfolio by $2.8\%$ of the achievable
range on every objective simultaneously, while optimal assembly on the
identical score and weights attains the reference set exactly.

The mechanism is the separation constraint.
Greedy commits to the highest-scoring site and then discards every site
within $d_{\min}$ of it, foreclosing combinations a global search
retains.
Because~\eqref{eq:rsep} is a hard constraint rather than an objective
\citep{Erkut1996,MoonChaudhry1984}, the foreclosure cannot be undone: given the score, no reweighting
restores a site greedy has already excluded.
This distinguishes the assembly effect from the normalisation effect:
the former is a combinatorial loss, the latter a re-parameterisation
of the value model.

\subsection{Isolating within-group distortion: a follow-up intervention}
\label{sec:e1}

Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst} predicts that between-group distortion
alone leaves exact assembly on the frontier.
We test this by intervening on the amplitudes directly.
The intervention was not part of the original pre-specified factorial
design but was specified as a follow-up analysis before any experimental
run; the predictions were committed to the repository before the first
run (Supplementary Material).
We therefore classify E1 as a \emph{follow-up analysis with
pre-specified predictions}, not as confirmatory evidence for the theorem
nor as fully post hoc exploratory.
Four score matrices differ only in $A_j$; objectives, frontier, draws and
feasible set are identical to the factorial ($\kappa=200$, $\ndraws$
draws, space $A'$).
\textbf{M} sets $A_j\equiv1$.
\textbf{B} sets $A_j=\bar A_g$, the weighted mean of $\rho_j$ within

vector normalisation with none within groups.

normalisation.

by a further $\EonePtwoDiff$~pp but reduces $R$ from $\RegretEoneW$
to $\RegrSAWvecOpt$~pp; its effect on combined distortion depends on the metric.

distortion matters in proportion to the weight its group carries in
the score, and that vector normalisation raises the weight of $G_1$,
the group of \textit{perm}, from $0.295$ to $0.544$.

becomes nearly lexicographic in siting cost, greedy and optimal choices
coincide in $62.8\%$ of draws, and the median greedy portfolio lies at
the $0.8$th percentile of $F_1$.

the frontier reliably through a near-lexicographic score comes at the cost
of selecting portfolios further from the value-optimal point.
The theorem says nothing about greedy assembly, and we do not treat
the $Q_2$ result as evidence for it.

\begin{remark}[TV$_W > 0$ is not sufficient for efficiency loss]
\label{rem:TVW}
Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst} is a necessary and sufficient condition for efficiency
\emph{over all instances}: $\mathrm{TV}_W=0$ iff exact assembly is Pareto-efficient
for every instance and every weight draw.
On any single instance, $\mathrm{TV}_W>0$ does not guarantee a loss, as
R-V$^*$ illustrates.
Its within-group amplitudes range from $0.482$ (\texttt{perm}) to $1.0$
(\texttt{roads}) in $G_1$, giving $\mathrm{TV}_W\approx0.082$ and
$\mathrm{TV}_B\approx0.072$, yet $Q_2=100.0\%$ under optimal assembly on
every draw.
E1-W, by contrast, has $\mathrm{TV}_W=\TVWvec$ and $\mathrm{TV}_B\approx0$
and loses $\EoneWloss$~pp under optimal assembly.
R-V$^*$ carries roughly $40\%$ of E1-W's within-group distortion and
loses nothing; the magnitude of $\mathrm{TV}_W$ alone therefore does not
determine loss.
Whether $\mathrm{TV}_W$ translates into loss depends on the structure of
the distortion. A plausible, untested mechanism is the direction of the
within-group distortion: E1-W inflates \textit{perm}, the most heavily
weighted criterion of $G_1$ (within-group share $\approx\IntraGperm\times$),
whereas R-V$^*$ deflates it (amplitude $0.482$ against $0.683$ and $1.0$
for the other $G_1$ criteria).
\end{remark}

\subsection{A pre-specified synthetic test of the TV\textsubscript{W} prediction}
\label{sec:e6}

Experiment~E1 establishes the cost of within-group distortion on
the Fogo instance (Section~\ref{sec:e1}).

predicts portfolio loss under different marginal distributions of the
criteria.

synthetic instances (\EsixNfam{} marginal families, 30 replicates each)
using the Fogo trio geometry and weight vector.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the aggregation contrast to weight dispersion}
\label{sec:kappa}

This is a post hoc sensitivity analysis, pre-registered before the E7 run
(PRESPEC\_E7\_KAPPA.md, amendments A1--A2, in the replication archive)
but specified after the main factorial results were known; all results are
descriptive.
Weights are drawn as $\mathbf{w}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\kappa\,\mathbf{w}_0)$;
smaller $\kappa$ means more dispersed weights
(for $w_{0,i}=0.2$, $\mathrm{SD}(w_i)$ falls from $0.078$ at $\kappa=25$
to $0.013$ at $\kappa=1000$).
Positive contrasts indicate higher attainment under SAW than under TOPSIS.

\textbf{Aggregation.}
The pooled SAW--TOPSIS contrast (three informative cells;
Section~\ref{sec:factorial}) decreases monotonically with~$\kappa$:
$\AggKtwentyfive$~pp at $\kappa=25$,
$\AggContrastKfifty$~pp at $\kappa=50$,
$\AggKonehundred$~pp at $\kappa=100$,
$\AggContrast$~pp at $\kappa=200$
(equivalent to zero; TOST $\delta=2.5$~pp, $p=\TOSTp$;
equivalence holds for any $\delta\ge1.9$~pp),
$\AggKfourhundred$~pp at $\kappa=400$,
and $\AggKthousand$~pp at $\kappa=1000$.
The sign changes between $\kappa=100$ and $\kappa=400$; the grid does not
locate the crossing more precisely.
All five non-null contrasts remain significant after Holm correction across
the six values of~$\kappa$.

Decomposing by method: the mean $Q_2$ across the three informative cells
falls from $\SAWmeanKtwentyfive\%$ (SAW, $\kappa=25$) to
$\SAWmeanKtwohundred\%$ ($\kappa=200$) and stabilises near~$70\%$ for
$\kappa\ge200$; the TOPSIS mean rises from $\TOPmeanKtwentyfive\%$ to
$\TOPmeanKthousand\%$ over the same range.
The reversal comes from both sides.

\textbf{Normalisation.}
The min--max advantage over vector normalisation (greedy assembly) is
$\NormKtwentyfive$~pp at $\kappa=25$, reaches a maximum near $\kappa=50$
($\NormGreedyContrastKfifty$~pp), and remains positive and significant
through $\kappa=400$ ($\NormKfourhundred$~pp); at $\kappa=1000$ it is
not distinguishable from zero ($\NormKthousand$~pp).
The normalisation effect is positive and significant at every tested
$\kappa$ up to~400; it is more stable than the aggregation effect.

\textbf{Assembly under vector normalisation.}
At $\kappa=25$ exact assembly improves $Q_2$ by $\AsmVecSAWKtwentyfiveMag$~pp
for SAW.
At $\kappa=200$, it reduces $Q_2$ by $\AsmVecSAW$~pp (SAW) and
$\AsmVecTOPSIS$~pp (TOPSIS) as reported in Section~\ref{sec:assembly}.
At $\kappa=1000$ the penalty reaches $\AsmVecSAWKthousandMag$~pp (SAW) and
$\AsmVecTOPKthousandMag$~pp (TOPSIS).
Aggregation is the design choice most sensitive to~$\kappa$; normalisation
is intermediate; the sign of the assembly effect also reverses with~$\kappa$.

All 36 bootstrap intervals are unadjusted (6 contrasts $\times$ 6~$\kappa$);
the analysis is post hoc and descriptive throughout.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The preceding results identify one mechanism of implicit re-weighting
(Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}) and one distinct failure mode (coding
mismatch outside the theorem's scope), which differ in cost, in
diagnostic, and in remedy.
This section interprets their relative severity, places them within the
portfolio decision analysis literature, and connects them to the broader
normalisation debate.

\subsection{One mechanism and one caveat, ordered by implication}

The results separate two routes by which a normalisation choice
degrades portfolio quality, and the distinction matters because the
remedies differ.

The first is \emph{implicit re-weighting}, characterised in closed form
by the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff}: an additive score built on normalised
columns of unequal amplitude operates on effective weights
$w^{\mathrm{eff}}\neq w$, whatever the analyst elicited.
The effect is graded, is summarised by the total
variation~\eqref{eq:TV}, and, on this instance, orders the three normalisations
monotonically within additive aggregation (Table~\ref{tab:tvorder}).
Within Fogo the ordering is monotone in $\mathrm{TV}$ under optimal
assembly (Table~\ref{tab:tvorder}), but Section~\ref{sec:e1} shows that
$\mathrm{TV}$ conflates two components with different consequences:
between-group distortion alone never moves the portfolio off the frontier
($Q_2=100\%$), but relocates it along the frontier at a mean cost of
$\RegretEoneB$~pp of value regret (median $\RegretEoneBMed$~pp);
within-group distortion carries
the $Q_2$ loss on this instance, with a cost of
$\EoneWloss$~pp under optimal assembly at $\kappa=200$.
It is also invisible: nothing in a standard TOPSIS or SAW report reveals
that \texttt{perm} entered at $\PermInflation$ times its nominal weight.

The caveat concerns \emph{score--evaluator coding mismatch}.
Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst} guarantees Pareto efficiency of exact
maximisation on every instance if and only if the score's amplitudes,
in the units of the objectives, are constant within each objective group.
Its premise fails when a criterion is encoded in the score by a function
that is not affine, or not even monotone, in the evaluator's partial value.
On Fogo, the coding inconsistency arose when aspect was scored as a
continuous azimuth while the objectives used the binary SE-facing indicator.
Crossing the two codings with the two reference sets
(Table~\ref{tab:coding2x2}) separates three effects.
The coding inconsistency alone costs $\RaspLoss$~pp under optimal assembly
($100.0\%$ to $\RaspOptimal\%$, 389-site constants).
Normalising over the $\Ncand$ candidates costs nothing under optimal
assembly when the coding is consistent ($100.0\%$ in both cells) and
improves greedy assembly by $\RefSetGreedyGain$~pp
($95\%$ CI $[\RoneTwoGrdCIlo,\,\RoneTwoGrdCIhi]$).
When the coding is inconsistent, the 473-site reference set reduces
$Q_2$ by $\ResidualRefOptDelta$~pp under optimal assembly relative to
the 389-site reference
($95\%$~CI $[\ResidualRefOptCIlo,\,\ResidualRefOptCIhi]$,
greedy: $\ResidualRefGrdDelta$~pp, $[\ResidualRefGrdCIlo,\,\ResidualRefGrdCIhi]$).
On this instance, a larger reference set under inconsistent coding
reduces $Q_2$ further; the sign and magnitude are not generalisable.
All cells in this paragraph are post hoc exploratory
(Supplementary Material).

The practical consequences differ.
Distributional re-weighting is a property of the data under a given
normalisation; it can be quantified and, if accepted, documented.
Criterion coding mismatch is avoidable: verifying that the score and
the objective function use the same encoding for each criterion before
applying exact optimisation removes it entirely at no additional cost.

\subsection{Relation to portfolio decision analysis}

The closest antecedents study portfolio construction from
multi-criteria scores.
\citet{BransMareschal1992} introduced constrained selection over
PROMETHEE flows; \citet{Mavrotas2006} combined multi-criteria scores
with binary programming; \citet{VetscheraDeAlmeida2012} formulated
$c$-optimal portfolios, of which the present problem with $k=\kk$ is an
instance; and \citet{DeAlmeidaVetschera2012} showed that PROMETHEE~V
portfolios depend on the scale transformation applied to the flows.

The last of these is the nearest precedent, and the difference is
instructive.
\citet{DeAlmeidaVetschera2012} establish that scale matters for one
method's portfolios, without an external reference against which to
measure the consequence.
We give a closed form for the induced re-weighting, applicable to any
additive aggregation and any normalisation, and measure the consequence
against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto set.
The robust portfolio modelling line \citep{Liesio2007,Liesio2008} and
SMAA-PO \citep{Yang2015SMAAPO} compute non-dominated portfolios exactly, but
in a scalar value space under weight uncertainty; our reference set
retains three unaggregated partial value functions, which permits the
dominance test to detect trade-off losses that a scalar criterion cannot.

Neither line imposes a hard pairwise separation constraint.
That constraint is standard in the location literature
\citep{MoonChaudhry1984,Erkut1996}, and it is what makes the assembly
effect combinatorial rather than parametric: a site foreclosed by greedy
selection cannot be recovered by any re-weighting
(Section~\ref{sec:greedy}).

\subsection{Relation to the normalisation literature}
\label{sec:normliterature}

That criterion weights cannot be interpreted independently of the
normalisation is established.
\citet{Choo1999} show that a weight is meaningful only relative to the
scale and range of the criterion it multiplies, and distinguish nominal
from effective weights; \citet{Milani2005} demonstrate empirically that
the choice of normalisation norm changes rankings, particularly under
TOPSIS.
The group structure on which Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst} rests is the
value tree whose effects on elicited weights are documented behaviourally
\citep{Weber1988,Poyhonen2001}: those studies show that the grouping of
attributes changes the weights elicited; the theorem shows that, given
the grouping, a normalisation may rescale whole groups but must leave
weight ratios within each group unchanged.
Set dependence has been documented for specific methods:
\citet{GarciaCascales2012} exhibit rank reversal under changes to the
alternative set, \citet{Kuo2017} identifies a failure of the closeness
index, and \citet{OpricovicTzeng2004} trace the linear-versus-vector
contrast between VIKOR and TOPSIS.

Our contribution is not the existence of the effect but its closed form
and its cost.
The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} expresses the effective weight as
$w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}\propto w_j A_j$ and identifies $A_j$ for each of
the three standard normalisations, which yields three results that the
qualitative statement does not: min--max is the unique weight-preserving
choice among them; every binary criterion loses approximately half its
effective weight relative to a tie-free continuous criterion in rank
space, irrespective of class balance; and VIKOR's invariance to
per-criterion affine transformation follows immediately from its ratio
form.
The affine invariance of VIKOR sharpens the contrast that
\citet{OpricovicTzeng2004} describe.
VIKOR is not merely less sensitive to normalisation: within the affine
family it is exactly invariant, because the scale $a_j$ and shift $b_j$
of each criterion cancel in the ratio $(f^{*}_j-f_{ij})/(f^{*}_j-f^{-}_j)$.
TOPSIS, by contrast, inherits the amplitude by analogy with exponent
$\alpha=2$ (Remark~\ref{prop:exponent}), and SAW by analogy with $\alpha=1$.
The three aggregation families therefore span the full range of exposure
to the distortion: immunity (VIKOR), linear amplification (SAW), and
quadratic-like amplification (TOPSIS, by analogy; Remark~\ref{rem:mm_not_neutral}).
Three observations previously treated separately become corollaries of
one identity: the vector-normalisation distortion
(Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}), the rank-space contraction
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:Arank_binary}), and the amplitude change from normalising
over inadmissible alternatives (Remark~\ref{rem:TVW}, Table~\ref{tab:coding2x2}).

The second difference is evidential.
Existing comparisons measure the consequence of a normalisation choice by
agreement between rankings
\citep{Jahan2015,Aytekin2021,Vafaei2018,Vafaei2022,Salabun2020Sym},
which establishes that outcomes differ without establishing that any of
them is worse.
We measure the consequence against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto set:
a loss of $\EoneVloss$~pp under optimal assembly in the probability of selecting a non-dominated
portfolio is a statement about decision quality, not about disagreement.

This leads us to qualify a standing recommendation.
\citet{Milani2005} advise vector normalisation on the grounds that it is
less sensitive to the scale and range of the raw data.
Our results indicate that the advantage is bought at a price:
insensitivity to unit artefacts is obtained by letting the distributional
shape of each criterion determine its amplitude, and hence its effective
weight.
Where weights carry no normative content the trade may be acceptable.
Where they encode elicited stakeholder judgements, as AHP weights do, a
normalisation that silently multiplies one criterion by $\PermInflation\times$ and
scales another by a factor of $\UrbanRatioVec$ is difficult to defend, and min--max
on the admissible set is preferable on precisely the grounds
\citet{Choo1999} articulate.

\subsection{The PROMETHEE--Borda identity}

Section~\ref{prop:p2} is a corollary of results established three
decades ago.
\citet{Bouyssou1992} characterises the net flow method axiomatically and
shows that it reduces to Copeland or Borda when the valued preference
relations are crisp; \citet{Marchant1996} extends the Borda equivalence
to valued relations.
With the usual preference function the relations are crisp by
construction, so the identity is implied.
We restate it to fix the affine constants and to record that it holds
exactly under arbitrary tie structure, including heterogeneous tie
patterns across criteria.
Ties can change criterion amplitudes relative to min--max values but do
not create disagreement between PROMETHEE~II and the weighted midrank
score when both use the same oriented midranks and the same weights.
Dividing by $N+1$ is a common scale factor and does not alter effective
weights; the distortion of \texttt{perm}'s effective weight to $0.616\times$
(amplitude $A_j=0.547$)
is caused by the $350$ tied zeros compressing its midrank range,
independently of the final rescaling constant.
Without the identity, that amplitude reduction would plausibly be
attributed to the aggregation operator rather than to the tie structure.

\subsection{Implications for practice}
\label{sec:practice}

First, verify that the score and the objective function use the same
encoding for each criterion before adding exact optimisation.
Screening criteria applied after normalisation leave the value scale
calibrated on alternatives that are unavailable; but more consequentially,
a criterion encoded differently in the score and the objectives can
collapse Pareto efficiency entirely even when the reference sets agree.
Normalising over the admissible set is the semantically correct default
(the reference set defines what ``best'' and ``worst'' mean for the
decision at hand), but it does not
protect against inconsistent criterion encodings.

Second, verify that the score's amplitudes, measured in the units of the
value model, are constant within each objective group before adding exact
optimisation.
Sharing a normalisation is sufficient but not necessary
(cell~$B$, Table~\ref{tab:e1}).
Integer programming on a mismatched score optimises the mismatch:
SAW assembly is guaranteed under min--max (Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}); TOPSIS gains $+\AsmMMtopsis$~pp; under vector normalisation exact assembly loses $\AsmVecSAW$~pp (SAW) and $\AsmVecTOPSIS$~pp (TOPSIS) at $\kappa=200$ (these effects are not distinguishable from zero at $\kappa=50$, see Table~\ref{tab:contrasts}), but achieves $\RegrDiffSAWvec$~pp (SAW) less value regret.

Third, report effective weights alongside elicited weights, and with
them the pair $(\mathrm{TV},\,m_{\mathrm{eff}})$
of~\eqref{eq:TV} and~\eqref{eq:meff}, which requires one line given
the normalised matrix.
The total variation alone is insufficient: it is the $\ell^1$ norm of
the deviation and is invariant to whether that deviation sits on one
criterion or on many, and Section~\ref{sec:e1} shows that only the within-group part of the
deviation costs efficiency under exact assembly; we therefore also
recommend reporting $\mathrm{TV}_B$ and $\mathrm{TV}_W$.
A report stating $\mathrm{TV}=0.31$ with $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=4$ on a
scale of $[2,11]$ tells the decision maker both that the elicited
weights were substantially moved and that the movement is concentrated;
neither number conveys this alone.
Where elicited weights carry normative content, as with AHP judgements
from stakeholders, the re-weighting implicit in vector normalisation is
difficult to defend (Section~\ref{sec:normliterature}).

Fourth, report the order in which orientation adjustment and
normalisation are applied.
Under min--max the order is immaterial because the complementation
$x\mapsto 1-x$ is affine.
Under vector normalisation it is not: complementing before normalising
gives amplitude $1/\lVert\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{x}\rVert_2$ while
normalising first gives $1/\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2$, and the two
normalised columns are not collinear (neither is a scalar multiple
of the other) (Corollary~\ref{cor:order}).
For a binary indicator with $n_1$ ones and $n_0$ zeros the amplitudes
differ by $\sqrt{n_0/n_1}$; for \textit{aspect} on Fogo this is $\AspectAmplitudeRatio$.
The order of operations is not reported in any study we reviewed, yet it
determines which column is normalised and therefore which amplitude
enters the effective weights.

Comparing aggregation operators, by contrast, produces a pooled contrast
equivalent to zero at $\kappa=200$ ($\AggContrast$~pp, TOST $p=\TOSTp$),
but rising to $\AggContrastKfifty$~pp at $\kappa=50$ (Table~\ref{tab:contrasts});
aggregation is the most $\kappa$-sensitive of the three design choices.
The recommendations above apply to SAW and TOPSIS: the amplitude enters
the score linearly for SAW and, by analogy, quadratically for TOPSIS.
VIKOR is invariant to per-criterion affine transformation by its ratio
construction, so the choice of normalisation within the affine family
(min--max, vector, sum) is irrelevant for VIKOR; effective weights
equal elicited weights regardless of $A_j$.
The recommendations to use a normalisation that matches the value model
and to report TV therefore apply
to additive and distance-based aggregation, not to compromise-programming
methods with ratio normalisation.

\subsection{Limitations}

\emph{Benchmark circularity.}
The Pareto frontier is enumerated using the same weighted-sum value
model that the methods under evaluation optimise.
The benchmark is therefore internal to the model class: it cannot detect
whether the value model is mis-specified.
The MAVT justification for the min--max choice is presented in Section~\ref{sec:design};
the robustness experiment (F8) is in Section~\ref{sec:alignment};
they confirm that alignment explains the diagonal cells, but the asymmetry
($\FEightVecMMOptLoss$~pp when min--max scores against a vector frontier
vs $\FEightMmVecOptLoss$~pp when vector scores against a min--max frontier)
indicates that min--max scores are more robust to frontier misspecification;
neither result resolves the deeper
limitation that $Q_2$ measures proximity to one value model.
A method that scores well on $Q_2$ may still select a portfolio that
would be suboptimal under a different specification of the objectives.

\emph{Scope of the closed form.}
The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} is exact for additive
aggregation under affine normalisation (min-max and vector);
for rank normalisation the identity is approximate.
Remark~\ref{prop:exponent} extends it to distance-based
families through the amplitude exponent, but the extension is
exact for the squared TOPSIS distance rather than the distance itself,
and we have not characterised families whose contribution is not
homogeneous in $w_j n_j(\cdot)$, outranking methods with non-linear
preference functions in particular.

\emph{Empirical support for the ordering.}
Under optimal assembly the three normalisation levels are ordered by
$\mathrm{TV}$ on Fogo (Table~\ref{tab:tvorder}), but three levels with
\textit{vector} carrying most of the regressor variance do not identify
a predictive coefficient.
The interval $\mathrm{TV}\in(0.10,\,0.30)$ contains no standard
normalisation: Weitendorf's linear transformation is algebraically
identical to min--max (for a cost criterion,
$(x_{\max}-x)/(x_{\max}-x_{\min})=1-(x-x_{\min})/(x_{\max}-x_{\min})$),
hence $\mathrm{TV}=0$.
Populating the interval requires manipulating the amplitude directly,
for instance along the path $A_j(t)=\rho_j^{\,t}$, $t\in[0,1]$, which
interpolates between min--max ($t=0$) and vector normalisation ($t=1$).
Further normalisation families and a synthetic grid varying the
marginal distributions are reported in the Supplementary Material.

\emph{Instance dependence.}
The analytic results hold for any instance; the magnitudes do not.
Fogo's deviation vector is two-sided and concentrated
($m_{\mathrm{eff}}=\meff$ on $[2,11]$), with one criterion inflated
$\PermInflation\times$, and the joint distribution of
$(\mathrm{TV}_B,\mathrm{TV}_W)$ in applied decision matrices is unknown;
establishing it would require a corpus of published matrices.
Two synthetic exercises bear on this only partially
(Supplementary Material).
A grid of 240 instances on the Fogo geometry shows that greedy-assembly
loss under vector normalisation tracks the magnitude--concentration pair
rather than $\mathrm{TV}$ alone, but does not calibrate the level of loss
outside its generating family.
A second grid of 405 instances ($N\le200$, three geometries;
Experiment~E5) imposes amplitudes on score and objectives jointly, so
optimal assembly is efficient by construction and the grid does not
test Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}; on greedy assembly, we found no
evidence of linear association between the imposed within-group spread
and greedy loss in the model as fitted
($-1.3$~pp per $0.1$ of $\mathrm{TV}_W$,
$95\%$ CI $[-5.1,\,+2.4]$), while instance size and spatial geometry
dominate (Supplementary Material, Experiment~E5).
E5 departs from its pre-specification in seven declared respects,
including the omission of the $N=389$ level (Supplementary Material).
External confirmation on other published instances was not obtained.

A pre-specified experiment, with declared deviations, on \EsixNinst{} synthetic instances
across \EsixNfam{} marginal families (Experiment~E6,
Section~\ref{sec:e6}) provides the most direct cross-family test of the
$\mathrm{TV}_W$ prediction; at equal $\mathrm{TV}_W$, losses ranged from below $3$~pp
under continuous marginals to $\EsixLossZinfWinfF$~pp under zero-inflation
($3$--$4\times$), confirming that $\mathrm{TV}_W$ alone does not calibrate the cost.
E5 found no linear association under greedy assembly ($-1.3$~pp, 95\%~CI
$[-5.1,\,+2.4]$).

Most landfill-selection studies in the literature apply continuous GIS
overlay analysis rather than enumerating a finite set of candidate sites
with numerical criterion scores; extracting a comparable decision matrix
would require constructing instances from publicly available spatial data,
which is left for future work.

\emph{Anchoring of the weights.}
Behavioural studies report that elicited weights adjust insufficiently to
attribute ranges \citep{vonNitzsch1993,Fischer1995}.
The anchoring of the AHP weights to $\mathcal{A}'$ assumed in
Section~\ref{sec:design} may therefore not hold; $Q_2$ then measures
attainment relative to the stated value model, not to the decision
maker's underlying preferences.

\emph{Weight perturbation model.}
Dirichlet perturbation around $\wahp$ represents symmetric uncertainty in
relative weights.
For structured disagreement among stakeholders,
SMAA-style weight-space analysis \citep{Lahdelma2002} would be
appropriate.
For robustness concepts that extend Pareto efficiency to uncertain data
rather than uncertain weights, \citet{Engau2020} characterise
non-dominated solutions under dominance relations derived from
distributional assumptions; our perturbation model is simpler but
sufficient for the present purpose of measuring how often each cell
recovers the nominal frontier.

\emph{Gap measure and weak dominance.}\;\label{sec:e1q3}
The shortfall $\mathrm{gap}(t)$ in~\eqref{eq:gap} takes the minimum
over strictly signed differences; if a dominator improves on $t$ in
only one or two objectives (with equality on the rest), the minimum
is zero and the gap does not detect the dominance.
A concrete instance: with three minimisation objectives, portfolio
$t=(3,5,5)$ is dominated by $t'=(3,5,4)$ (strict improvement on
objective~3, ties elsewhere), yet $\mathrm{gap}(t)=\min(0,0,1)=0$.
$Q_2$ and $Q_3$ therefore measure different aspects of efficiency
and need not agree: a cell may attain the frontier often ($Q_2$ high)
while falling far below it when it misses ($Q_3$ high).
$Q_2$, computed from the full Pareto dominance algorithm, is the
primary efficiency measure; $Q_3$ is a complementary severity indicator.
Both are computed against the same reference set $\mathcal{F}(w)$.
On Fogo, $\mathrm{gap}=0$ coincided with Pareto membership on every
draw in all eight cells, so weak dominance did not affect the reported values.
$Q_3$ for all cells is reported in Table~\ref{tab:factorial}.

\emph{Regulatory grounding.}
The $\Nexcl$ candidates overlapping protected areas are excluded before
analysis; the exclusion removes them from the selectable set and does not affect the
normalisation reference set comparison in Table~\ref{tab:coding2x2}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =======================================================================

We evaluated ranking-based multi-criteria methods for multi-site landfill
selection against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier of
$\Ntrios$ portfolios under a hard spatial separation constraint.
The design crossed aggregation, normalisation and assembly, and measured
how often each combination recovers a Pareto-efficient portfolio under
perturbation of the elicited weights.

The choice of aggregation operator, the variable the applied literature
compares most, had a pooled mean equivalent to zero on Fogo at $\kappa=200$ ($\AggContrast$~pp,
$95\%$ CI $[\AggLo,\,\AggHi]$, TOST $p=\TOSTp$; at $\kappa=50$: $\AggContrastKfifty$~pp,
$[+\AggLoKfifty,\,+\AggHiKfifty]$);
the sign ranged from $\AggStratumMMgrd$~pp to $+\AggStratumVecopt$~pp
depending on the cell, confirming a cell-dependent interaction rather
than an absence of effect.
Normalisation was worth $+\NormGreedyContrast$~pp under greedy assembly
on Fogo.
Exact assembly had no unconditional benefit on this instance: the value depends on the normalisation. SAW assembly was guaranteed under min--max (Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt})
and TOPSIS gained $+\AsmMMtopsis$~pp at $\kappa=200$; under vector normalisation exact assembly
lost $\AsmVecSAW$~pp (SAW) and $\AsmVecTOPSIS$~pp (TOPSIS) at $\kappa=200$ in frontier
attainment to greedy assembly (both effects not distinguishable from zero at $\kappa=50$), but achieved $\RegrDiffSAWvec$~pp (SAW)
less value regret (below the $\delta=2.5$~pp equivalence margin).
The explanation is analytical: in additive aggregation the effective
weight of a criterion is proportional to $w_jA_j$, and exact assembly
is Pareto-efficient for any weights and any instance of the additive model if and only if
amplitudes are constant within each objective group
(Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst}).
A follow-up intervention with pre-specified predictions on Fogo illustrated,
as Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst} guarantees,
that between-group distortion alone never moves the portfolio off the frontier
($Q_2=100\%$) but costs $\RegretEoneB$~pp of value regret, and
that within-group distortion carries
most of the loss; the prediction that the two components would not
interact was not confirmed (P2 point estimate exceeds the 5~pp threshold;
the confidence interval includes values below it).
A synthetic grid of 405 instances (Experiment~E5) characterises the
greedy shortfall rather than the normalisation effect: with amplitudes
imposed jointly on score and objectives, we found no evidence of linear
association between the imposed amplitude spread and greedy efficiency
in the model as fitted; greedy efficiency was more strongly associated
with instance size and spatial geometry.

A pre-specified experiment on \EsixNinst{} synthetic instances across
\EsixNfam{} marginal families (Experiment~E6, Section~\ref{sec:e6})
supports the directional prediction and shows that at equal $\mathrm{TV}_W$
the cost ranges $3$--$4\times$ across marginal families, confirming that $\mathrm{TV}_W$
alone does not calibrate its magnitude.

The Fogo magnitudes are therefore instance-specific; the within-group
mechanism is directionally robust but its magnitude depends on the
marginal distribution of criterion values.

The magnitudes in percentage points of $Q_2$ measure how often a design
choice leaves the frontier, not how far: on this instance the shortfall,
when it occurs, is $1$--$3\%$ of the attainable range
(Table~\ref{tab:factorial}).

Two consequences bear directly on practice.
In the Fogo factorial, exact assembly improved Pareto attainment under
min--max and reduced it under vector normalisation at $\kappa=200$, relative to the
min--max value model; under vector normalisation it performs worse than
reading the ranking top-down at $\kappa=200$ (effects not distinguishable from zero at $\kappa=50$).
A criterion encoded differently in the score and the objectives
reduces $Q_2$ from $100\%$ to $\RaspOptimal\%$ under optimal assembly
on Fogo; with a consistent encoding, normalising over inadmissible
alternatives costs nothing under optimal assembly on this instance.
The immediate recommendation is inexpensive: report effective weights
alongside elicited ones.
Given the normalised decision matrix the computation is a single line,
and it exposes distortions that rank-agreement diagnostics do not reveal.
Where weights encode stakeholder judgements, as AHP weights do,
the re-weighting is difficult to defend; the argument is developed in
Section~\ref{sec:normliterature}.

The closed form is exact for additive aggregation under affine normalisation; for ranks the identity is approximate. TOPSIS combines distances to ideal points, where amplitudes enter quadratically, and extending the exact characterisation to TOPSIS (via the $\alpha=2$ analogy) remains open.
Theorem~\ref{thm:groupconst} is an exact characterisation for additive
aggregation; the ordering of cells by $Q_2$ under combined distortions
observed in Section~\ref{sec:e1} (between-group does not simply amplify
within-group by the $R$ metric, but the $Q_2$ ordering places V below W)
is measured on one instance and not yet explained analytically.
The magnitudes are specific to one candidate set: the analytical results
hold for any instance, but the size of the distortion depends on the
marginal distributions, and a criterion with $350$ tied zeros among
$\Nvia$ sites is an adversarial case; extending the measurement to
further instances remains open.

The reference-set analyses in Section~\ref{sec:alignment} and
Section~\ref{sec:coding} are based on post hoc exploratory cells
(Supplementary Material) and should be
treated as descriptive rather than confirmatory.
A post hoc analysis found that
greedy assembly improves by $+\RefSetGreedyGain$~pp under consistent
coding ($95\%$ CI $[\RoneTwoGrdCIlo,\,\RoneTwoGrdCIhi]$); under optimal
assembly both cells reach $100\%$ on the sampled draws, so the estimated
contrast was zero on this instance (an observation on Fogo, not a
general zero-cost guarantee for mismatched reference sets).

The broader implication is methodological.
Benchmarking multi-criteria methods by rank agreement
can establish that they differ, but not that any is closer to the
decision maker's objectives.
Where the feasible set is enumerable, as it is for
cardinality-constrained siting problems of realistic size, an exact
frontier provides an external standard, and under that standard the
ranking of design choices is not the one the literature's emphasis would
suggest at the weight dispersion used as primary; at $\kappa=50$ aggregation
matters substantially.
