\begin{abstract}
Multi-site facility siting is a portfolio problem: several locations must
be chosen jointly, subject to a minimum separation between them.
Practice instead relies on multi-criteria rankings read top-down, and the
methodological literature compares such rankings by measuring their
agreement with one another, which establishes that methods differ, but
not that any is worse.
We supply an optimality benchmark that is internal to the value model but
external to the ranking.
For $\Ncand$ candidate sites on Fogo Island, Cape Verde, we enumerate the
complete feasible set of $\Ntrios$ three-site portfolios under a
$\dmin$~km separation constraint and compute the exact Pareto frontier
over siting efficiency, environmental integrity and social safety.
Crossing four aggregation methods with three normalisations and two
assembly rules, we find that the choice between SAW and TOPSIS is worth
$+0.9$ percentage points ($95\%$ CI $[-0.5,\,+2.3]$), whereas
normalisation is worth $+29.9$ and assembly $+16.0$.
We explain this analytically: in additive aggregation the effective
weight of a criterion is proportional to $w_j A_j$, where $A_j$ is the
amplitude of the normalised column, so normalisation fixes the operative
weights upstream of the aggregation operator.
The distortion is governed by an amplitude exponent that differs across
aggregation families: min--max preserves elicited weights under additive
aggregation but not under TOPSIS, where amplitudes enter quadratically
and $\mathrm{TV}=0.160$ even when all $A_j\equiv 1$.
Min--max is the unique standard normalisation that preserves elicited
weights under SAW; vector normalisation multiplies one criterion's weight
by $2.88\times$ in our data; every binary criterion loses half its weight
in rank space.
Exact optimisation inherits the distortion, and normalising over
inadmissible alternatives costs up to $80$ percentage points of Pareto
efficiency.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Normalisation implicitly re-weights criteria: effective weight
  scales with $w_jA_j$
\item Min--max is the only standard normalisation that preserves
  elicited weights under additive aggregation (SAW)
\item Normalisation is worth $+29.9$~pp of Pareto efficiency; aggregation

\item Exact assembly gains $+29.8$~pp under min--max but only $+2.2$~pp

\item Normalising over inadmissible sites costs up to $80$~pp of Pareto

\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =======================================================================

Siting a waste facility is rarely a matter of choosing one location.
Regional plans allocate several landfills at once, and the sites must be
spatially dispersed: co-locating them concentrates traffic, leachate risk
and public opposition in one area, and defeats the purpose of a
distributed network.
The decision is therefore a \emph{portfolio} problem: choose $\kk$
sites out of $N$ subject to a minimum pairwise separation, rather than
a ranking problem.

Practice, however, is organised around rankings.
The standard workflow scores candidate sites with a multi-criteria method,
reads the ranking top-down, and skips any site too close to one already
chosen.
In a review of 114 landfill siting studies, \citet{Rezaeisabzevar2020}
found TOPSIS, SAW and AHP to be the dominant tools, and normalisation
choices to be rarely reported.
The methodological literature compares these methods extensively, but
almost always by measuring agreement between the rankings they produce
\citep{Chakraborty2009,Jahan2015,Vafaei2018,Aytekin2021,Salabun2020Sym}.
Agreement statistics establish that methods disagree; they cannot
establish that any one of them is worse, because there is no external
standard against which to judge.

This paper supplies such a standard and uses it to ask which design
choice in the workflow actually matters.
For a real candidate set of $\Ncand$ sites on Fogo Island, Cape Verde,
we enumerate the complete feasible set of $\Ntrios$ three-site portfolios
satisfying a $d_{\min}=\dmin$~km separation constraint, and compute the
exact Pareto frontier over three group-level objectives: siting efficiency,
environmental integrity and social safety.
Because the frontier is obtained by exhaustive enumeration rather than by
a metaheuristic, a portfolio produced by any method can be classified as
Pareto-efficient or not, with no approximation error.
We then cross four aggregation methods with three normalisations and two
assembly rules, and measure how often each cell recovers an efficient
portfolio under Monte Carlo perturbation of the elicited weights.

The answer is not about the aggregation methods.
Choosing between SAW and TOPSIS is worth $+0.9$ percentage points (pp)
with a $95\%$ interval of $[-0.5,\,+2.3]$, statistically
indistinguishable from no effect.
The choice of normalisation is worth $+29.9$~pp, and the choice between
greedy and exact assembly $+16.0$~pp.

The reason is a mechanism that operates upstream of the aggregation
operator, and it is the analytical core of the paper.
That a criterion weight cannot be interpreted independently of the scale
on which the criterion is measured has been understood since
\citet{Choo1999}, who distinguished nominal from effective weights and
argued that the latter governs the outcome.
We state this relation in closed form: in any additive aggregation, the
effective weight of criterion $j$ is $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} \propto w_j
A_j$, where $A_j$ is the amplitude of the normalised column.
Three consequences follow immediately, and none is available from the
qualitative statement.
Min--max normalisation has $A_j \equiv 1$ and is the unique
weight-preserving choice among the three standard families.
Vector normalisation has $A_j = \rho_j$, determined by the distributional
shape of the criterion, which in our data multiplies one criterion's
weight by $2.88\times$ and divides another by $0.53\times$
(\textit{urban}) without any of this appearing in the
analyst's report.
Midranks rescaled to the unit interval contract with tie mass, and every
binary criterion loses half its weight in rank space irrespective of
class balance.

The mechanism also explains the interaction we observe between
normalisation and assembly.
Exact optimisation of an additive score is guaranteed to yield a
Pareto-efficient portfolio under the weights that score encodes.
When the score encodes $w^{\mathrm{eff}} \neq w$, exact optimisation
solves the wrong problem exactly: replacing greedy with optimal assembly
is worth $+29.8$~pp under min--max normalisation and $+2.2$~pp under
vector normalisation, with an interval containing zero.
Adding integer programming on top of a ranking, the established
upgrade path
\citep{BransMareschal1992,Mavrotas2006,VetscheraDeAlmeida2012}, pays
only when the normalisation is already consistent with the value model.

A third finding concerns the set over which normalisation constants are
computed.
In our data, $\Nexcl$ of the $\Ncand$ candidates overlap protected areas
and cannot be selected.
Computing the value scale over all $\Ncand$ nonetheless calibrates it on
alternatives that are unavailable, and the resulting mismatch between the
scale used by the method and the scale defining the objectives costs up
to $80$~pp of Pareto efficiency, far more than the re-weighting alone
predicts.
The loss is also strongly asymmetric, which tells us the two mechanisms
are distinct rather than two readings of one effect.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item A closed-form characterisation of normalisation as implicit
    re-weighting (the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff}), formalising a relation
    established qualitatively by \citet{Choo1999}, with the amplitude
    $A_j$ identified for min--max, vector and rank normalisation
    (Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}).
    Three effects previously reported separately, namely vector-normalisation
    distortion, tie-induced contraction in rank space, and amplitude loss
    from normalising over inadmissible alternatives, become corollaries
    of one identity.
  \item A measurement of the cost of that distortion in units of Pareto
    efficiency rather than rank agreement, against an exhaustively
    enumerated frontier of $\Ntrios$ portfolios under a hard separation
    constraint.
  \item A factorial decomposition showing that normalisation and assembly
    dominate the choice of aggregation operator, with an interaction that
    the main effects conceal, and an explanation of why: the distortion
    acts through the weighted sum and the aggregation operator lies
    downstream of it.
  \item An exact identity between PROMETHEE~II under the usual preference
    function and rank-weighted SAW, holding for arbitrary tie structure
    (Section~\ref{prop:p2}), which follows from the axiomatic results
    of \citet{Bouyssou1992} and \citet{Marchant1996} and which isolates
    normalisation as the sole source of the divergence observed in
    practice.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:litreview}

Two bodies of work bear directly on this paper: the portfolio-assembly literature,
which treats the selection of a cardinality-constrained set as a combinatorial
problem, and the normalisation literature, which documents the effect of
scale transformations on multi-criteria scores.
The review is selective; it focuses on results that are directly used or challenged
in the remainder.
% =======================================================================

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Portfolio construction from MCDM rankings}
\label{sec:lit_portfolio}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The dominant paradigm for multi-facility siting combines an MCDM score
with a combinatorial rule.
\citet{BransMareschal1992} introduced PROMETHEE~V, in which PROMETHEE~II
flows serve as coefficients of a binary programme, and produced the first
exact solution for a portfolio problem from a ranking method.
\citet{Mavrotas2006} and \citet{Mavrotas2008} extended this to integer
programmes under budget and logical constraints, demonstrating that the
quality of the resulting portfolio depends on how closely the programme
matches the method's underlying value model.
\citet{VetscheraDeAlmeida2012} formalised $c$-optimal portfolios under
PROMETHEE~II, showing that the portfolio with exactly $c$~facilities that
maximises a PROMETHEE-derived score is sensitive to the score scale.
\citet{DeAlmeidaVetschera2012} isolated this scale sensitivity:
transforming the PROMETHEE flows before insertion into the integer
programme changes the optimal portfolio, establishing that the selection
step is not scale-invariant.

Parallel work in portfolio decision analysis (PDA) approached the problem
from a different direction.
\citet{Liesio2007} and \citet{Liesio2008} proposed Robust Portfolio
Modelling (RPM), which computes portfolios that are non-dominated in a
scalar value space over a region of uncertain weights, using an
interactive linear programme.
\citet{Mild2015} applied RPM to R\&D portfolio selection; \citet{Yang2015SMAAPO}
combined SMAA with portfolio optimisation (SMAA-PO), producing
acceptability indices for portfolios under full weight uncertainty.
\citet{Liesio2021} reviewed 148 PDA contributions and found that
cardinality-constrained portfolio selection with spatial interdependencies
between facilities remains largely unexplored.

The present paper occupies a position the PDA tradition does not fill:
the three objectives $F_1, F_2, F_3$ are kept as separate partial value
functions (not collapsed into one score), and the reference set is an
exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier under a hard pairwise spatial
separation constraint.
RPM and SMAA-PO treat weight uncertainty around a single scalar;
this paper treats decomposition by objective group and measures how much
of the resulting Pareto frontier each method--normalisation--assembly
combination recovers.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Normalisation and effective weights}
\label{sec:lit_norm}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

That a criterion weight cannot be interpreted independently of the scale
used to normalise that criterion has been known since at least
\citet{Choo1999}, who demonstrated that nominal and effective weights
diverge whenever criterion columns have different ranges after
normalisation, and argued that the effective weight is the operationally
relevant quantity.
\citet{Milani2005} confirmed this empirically, showing that different
normalisation norms produce different rankings in a gear material
selection study and recommending vector normalisation on the grounds that
it is less sensitive to the raw scale of the data.

A large subsequent literature has measured the sensitivity of rankings to
normalisation choice, principally within TOPSIS.
\citet{Chakraborty2009} introduced the Ranking Consistency Index (RCI)
and, through simulation, found vector normalisation the most internally
consistent choice for TOPSIS; \citet{Vafaei2018} corroborated this for
TOPSIS and \citet{Vafaei2022} extended the finding to SAW.
\citet{Jahan2015} surveyed 31 normalisation techniques and emphasised
that apparently minor differences can produce materially different
engineering decisions.
\citet{Aytekin2021} compared 14 techniques across 14 data structures and
found that no single technique dominates across all settings.

Two observations qualify this body of work.
First, every study uses ranking agreement (RCI, Spearman's $\rho$,
or Pearson correlation between ranked lists) as its performance measure,
which establishes that outcomes differ without establishing that any
outcome is better.
Second, the recommendation of vector normalisation rests on internal
consistency with respect to the TOPSIS ideal-distance score; it does not
account for the fact that distributional shape of the criterion columns
determines the effective weight (the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff}), which may
conflict with elicited stakeholder judgements.
This paper supplies the missing dimension: a comparison against an
exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier, which measures decision quality
directly.

Set dependence of normalisation has also been documented for specific
methods.
\citet{GarciaCascales2012} showed that TOPSIS rankings reverse when the
alternative set changes, and recommended scales independent of the set.
\citet{Kuo2017} identified a structural failure in the TOPSIS closeness
index under vector normalisation.
\citet{OpricovicTzeng2004} traced the linear-versus-vector contrast
between VIKOR and TOPSIS to normalisation choices with consequences for
unit invariance.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Theory of the methods}
\label{sec:lit_methods}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The four methods evaluated here are standard.
\citet{Hwang1981} introduced TOPSIS and SAW; the original PROMETHEE
formulation is due to \citet{Brans1986}.
For VIKOR we follow \citet{OpricovicTzeng2004}.
The connection between PROMETHEE~II and Borda scoring is established
by the axiomatic results of \citet{Bouyssou1992} and
\citet{Marchant1996}.
\citet{Bouyssou1992} characterised the net flow as a scoring method
subsuming Borda and Copeland when preferences are crisp;
\citet{Marchant1996} extended the equivalence to valued preference
relations, which is the setting of PROMETHEE~II.
Section~\ref{prop:p2} exploits this connection to provide an exact
algebraic identity between the rank-normalised SAW score and the
PROMETHEE~II net flow.

Rank reversal and set dependence in PROMETHEE~II have been studied by
\citet{Mareschal2008}, \citet{Dejaegere2022}, and \citet{Coquelet2024},
who formalised the mechanism by which a third alternative shifts the
flows of existing alternatives.
These results concern a different channel than the one we study: our
set-dependence effect (Section~\ref{sec:refset}) operates through
normalisation constants, not through pairwise preference flows.

\citet{Eppe2014} examined the computational scalability of exact
PROMETHEE ranking for candidate sets of the size used here ($|\Tset|$
up to several hundred candidates), confirming tractability.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Benchmarking MCDM methods}
\label{sec:lit_bench}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

Systematic benchmarking of MCDM methods has expanded substantially.
\citet{Cinelli2020} reviewed frameworks for selecting among methods and
argued for explicit documentation of the assumptions embedded in each
choice.
\citet{Salabun2020Sym} compared TOPSIS, VIKOR, COPRAS, and
PROMETHEE~II using stochastic simulations and Spearman correlation,
confirming that methods agree moderately under standard settings but
diverge when normalisation or weighting changes.
\citet{PintoCosta2005} proposed weighted rank correlation coefficients
for comparing decision matrices; \citet{Watrobski2019} developed a
framework for method selection incorporating data complexity diagnostics.

All of these benchmarks use rank agreement as their criterion of
comparison.
The present paper adds an external standard: a reference set of
Pareto-optimal portfolios computed without any MCDM aggregation.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Spatial constraints and undesirable facilities}
\label{sec:lit_spatial}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The theory of facility location under spatial separation constraints
originates in the \emph{r-separation problem}:
\citet{MoonChaudhry1984} first formalised the problem of selecting
facilities subject to a minimum pairwise distance constraint in the
operations research literature.
\citet{Erkut1989} reviewed siting models for undesirable facilities and
noted that MCDM methods are widely used in practice but rarely compared
against exact optimisation benchmarks.
\citet{Erkut1996} proposed integer-friendly MIP formulations for
r-separation that make exact solution tractable for problems of the size
considered here.
\citet{Kuby1987} studied the related p-dispersion problem (maximise the
minimum inter-facility distance); \citet{Church2022} reviewed the
broader class of dispersion and diversity maximisation models.
\citet{Marti2022} provided the most recent comprehensive review of
discrete dispersion maximisation from an operations research perspective.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Landfill site selection}
\label{sec:lit_landfill}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

MCDM methods are the dominant tool for landfill site selection in the
GIS-integrated literature.
\citet{Rezaeisabzevar2020} reviewed 114 studies and found that TOPSIS,
SAW, and AHP dominate, with normalisation choices rarely reported.
\citet{Korucu2012} reviewed the field critically and identified the
absence of spatial interdependencies among selected sites as a gap.
\citet{Spigolon2018} combined MCDA with mixed-integer quadratic
programming for multi-facility selection, but without comparing methods.
\citet{EiseltMarianov2014} are, to our knowledge, the only study to
compute an exact Pareto frontier for a landfill siting problem; they use
a bi-objective MIP without any MCDM ranking component, and the frontier
is bivariate rather than trivariate.
\citet{AzancortNeto2026} provide the spatial dataset used in the present
paper: $\Ncand$ candidate centroids on Fogo Island validated against a
complete exhaustive search of the feasible combinatorial space, with
AHP-derived weights and an exact Pareto frontier serving as the
multi-objective ground truth.
Precursor analyses of this dataset appear in \citet{AzancortNeto2021}
(decision-tree classification of site suitability) and \citet{Costa2020}
(mathematical and computational modelling of the AHP criteria matrix).
No study in this literature has compared ranking-based MCDM methods
against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier under a hard spatial
separation constraint while systematically varying aggregation,
normalisation, and assembly strategy.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The present contribution}
\label{sec:lit_gap}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

Drawing together the observations above, the gap is as follows.
The PDA tradition computes exact portfolios in a scalar value space but
does not decompose objectives into groups or impose hard spatial
separation.
The normalisation literature documents sensitivity of rankings to
normalisation choice but measures it by internal agreement, not by an
external quality standard.
The benchmarking literature compares methods by rank agreement.
The landfill literature uses MCDM rankings without comparing them to any
optimality benchmark.

The present paper addresses this gap by: (i)~enumerating the exact Pareto
frontier of $\Ntrios$ portfolios over three group-level partial value
functions under $r$-separation; (ii)~evaluating four aggregation methods crossed with
three normalisations and two assembly strategies against that frontier;
and (iii)~providing a closed-form account of the mechanism by which
normalisation implicitly re-weights criteria, expressed in terms
established by \citet{Choo1999} and measured in terms of Pareto
efficiency loss.

In the literature reviewed (55 studies, including the 148-article PDA
survey of \citet{Liesio2021}), no prior work evaluates ranking-based
MCDM portfolios against an exhaustive Pareto frontier under hard spatial
separation, crosses aggregation, normalisation, and assembly as a
factorial, or measures the cost of normalisation choice in units of
Pareto efficiency.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

This section defines the siting problem formally and introduces the three evaluation
axes, the separation constraint, and the Pareto frontier against which all
method--normalisation--assembly combinations are subsequently measured.

\subsection{Candidate sites and the separation constraint}

Let $S=\{1,\dots,N\}$ index candidate sites, each described by $m=11$
geospatial criteria.
A \emph{portfolio} is a subset $t\subseteq S$ with $|t|=\kk$.
Portfolios must satisfy a hard pairwise separation constraint
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rsep}
  d(u,v) \;\ge\; d_{\min}=\dmin\;\text{km}
  \qquad \forall\, u,v\in t,\; u\neq v,
\end{equation}
where $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the great-circle distance.
Constraint~\eqref{eq:rsep} is the $r$-separation condition of
\citet{Erkut1996}, whose network antecedent is \citet{MoonChaudhry1984}.
We emphasise that dispersion enters here as a \emph{constraint}, not an
objective: the $p$-dispersion literature \citep{Kuby1987,Marti2022}
maximises the minimum pairwise distance, whereas~\eqref{eq:rsep} merely
renders non-compliant portfolios infeasible.
The feasible set is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:T}
  \Tset = \bigl\{\,t\subseteq S \;:\; |t|=\kk,\ t \text{ satisfies }
    \eqref{eq:rsep}\,\bigr\},
  \qquad |\Tset| = \Ntrios.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Three partial value functions}

The $m$ criteria are partitioned into three groups reflecting the
decision dimensions of landfill siting: siting efficiency ($G_1$),
environmental integrity ($G_2$), and social safety ($G_3$)
(Table~\ref{tab:amplitude}).
Let $c_j(i)\in[0,1]$ be the partial value of site~$i$ on criterion~$j$,
\emph{oriented so that larger is better} --- cost and impact criteria are
complemented at normalisation --- and let $w_j$ be the criterion weight.
All three objectives are therefore maximised, and are additive within
group and across sites:
\begin{align}
  F_1(t) &= \sum_{i\in t}\sum_{j\in G_1} w_j\,c_j(i),
    && \text{(siting efficiency, maximise)} \label{eq:F1}\\
  F_2(t) &= \sum_{i\in t}\sum_{j\in G_2} w_j\,c_j(i),
    && \text{(environmental integrity, maximise)} \label{eq:F2}\\
  F_3(t) &= \sum_{i\in t}\sum_{j\in G_3} w_j\,c_j(i),
    && \text{(social safety, maximise)} \label{eq:F3}
\end{align}
and we write $\mathbf{F}(t;w)=(F_1,F_2,F_3)$ to make the weight
dependence explicit, since it is central to Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\paragraph{Why all three are maximised.}
Each criterion enters as a distance from an undesirable feature or as
its complement where the raw variable is a negative indicator, so that
larger is better throughout: \textit{perm} is distance from
low-permeability soil, \textit{urban} is distance from populated areas,
and so on.
The three group objectives therefore inherit a uniform larger-is-better
orientation, which makes the identity
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:scalarisation}
  \sum_{i\in t} s(i) \;=\; F_1(t)+F_2(t)+F_3(t),
  \qquad s(i)=\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\,c_j(i),
\end{equation}
exact rather than sign-dependent, and Section~\ref{prop:p1} turns on it.
Equation~\eqref{eq:scalarisation} also makes explicit that the SAW score
is the equally weighted scalarisation of the three group objectives; the
grouping carries no additional preference information beyond $w$.

\paragraph{What the benchmark is, and is not.}
\label{rem:valuespace}
$\mathbf{F}$ is a three-dimensional partial value space, not a vector of
physically independent objectives: each component is an additive weighted
aggregate of normalised criteria.
This distinguishes our reference set from portfolio decision analysis
approaches that compute non-dominated portfolios in a scalar value space
\citep{Liesio2007,Liesio2008,Yang2015SMAAPO}, and from bi-objective mixed-integer
landfill models whose objectives are physical quantities
\citep{EiseltMarianov2014}.
We keep the three group-level value functions unaggregated and ask how
much of the resulting trade-off surface is lost when a ranking method
collapses them into a single score before the portfolio is assembled.

\subsection{Reference set and the optimality gap}

Portfolio $t$ \emph{dominates} $t'$ when $F_\ell(t)\ge F_\ell(t')$ for
all $\ell\in\{1,2,3\}$, with at least one strict inequality.
Because $|\Tset|$ is finite and enumerable, the non-dominated set
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:front}
  \Fset(w) = \bigl\{\,t\in\Tset \;:\; \nexists\,t'\in\Tset
    \text{ dominating } t\,\bigr\}
\end{equation}
is obtained \emph{exactly}, with no metaheuristic approximation.
All dominance tests use a tolerance $\varepsilon=10^{-9}$.

For dominated portfolios we report a scale-free shortfall.
With $R_\ell = \max_{t\in\Tset}F_\ell - \min_{t\in\Tset}F_\ell$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gap}
  \mathrm{gap}(t) = \max\Bigl(0,\;\max_{t'\in\Tset}\;\min_{\ell\in\{1,2,3\}}
    \frac{F_\ell(t')-F_\ell(t)}{R_\ell}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
so $\mathrm{gap}(t)=0$ iff $t\in\Fset$.
Normalising by the attainable range rather than the ideal--nadir distance
keeps the measure interpretable as a fraction of the achievable spread;
the ideal--nadir variant is reported in the Supplementary Material.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The analysis is conducted on a GIS dataset for Fogo Island, Cape Verde.
This section describes the candidate grid, the criteria, and the preprocessing
decisions that affect the downstream amplitude calculations.
Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarises the six-stage pipeline; the subsections
that follow correspond to Stages~1--6 in order.

\subsection{Data and preprocessing}

Stage~1 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. The dataset comprises $\Ncand$ candidate sites on Fogo Island, Cape Verde,
screened from land-cover exclusion, each with $m=11$ criteria measured in
metres, except slope (dimensionless gradient) and aspect (degrees).
The spatial discretisation and criteria definition follow
\citet{AzancortNeto2026}, which provides the validated GIS dataset; the
present paper uses the same candidate set, pairwise distance matrix, and
criterion evaluations.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/fig_F4_map.pdf}
\caption{%
  Spatial layout of the $\Nvia$ admissible candidate sites on Fogo Island
  (grey dots).
  Blue diamonds mark the best-compromise portfolio
  $\{184,\,430,\,460\}$ returned by SAW under min--max normalisation with
  optimal assembly; dashed circles show the $d_{\min}=\dmin$~km separation
  radius around each selected site, confirming pairwise feasibility.
  The $\Nexcl$ candidates overlapping protected areas are excluded before
  analysis and are not shown.}
\label{fig:map}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Missing values.}
Three entries are missing: roads (site~41), urban distance (site~385), and
aerodromes (site~429).
Each is imputed by the midpoint of the observed range of its criterion over
all $\Ncand$ candidates.
Because the affected sites are three out of $\Ncand$ and none enters any
reported portfolio, the choice of imputation rule is immaterial.

\paragraph{Regulatory exclusion.}
Sites overlapping a protected area (\texttt{dist\_protected}$=0$) cannot
be licensed and are removed, leaving $\Nvia=\Ncand-\Nexcl$ admissible
sites.


\paragraph{Criterion orientation.}
Nine criteria are monotone distances; slope is a monotone cost.
Slope aspect is circular and non-monotone; following the source study, it
enters as an indicator: $c=1$ if aspect $\notin[90^\circ,180^\circ]$ and
$c=0$ otherwise, yielding $n_1=94$ positives among the $\Nvia$ admissible
sites.
This binarisation matters for Section~\ref{prop:p3}
and is recorded explicitly in Table~\ref{tab:amplitude}.

\subsection{Criterion weights}

Stage~2 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. Weights are obtained by AHP \citep{Saaty1990,Saaty2008} from the pairwise
comparison matrix of the source study \citep{Andrade2015,AzancortNeto2026}, by column
normalisation.
Consistency is acceptable: $\lambda_{\max}=\lmax$, $CI=0.08525$,
$RI=1.51$, hence $CR=\CR<0.10$.
The weight vector $\wahp$ is held fixed in the base experiment and
perturbed in the Monte Carlo design of Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\subsection{Normalisation}

Stage~3 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} is the analytical focus of the paper.
Three normalisations are compared, spanning the standard taxonomies of
\citet{Jahan2015} and \citet{Aytekin2021}:
\begin{align}
  \text{min--max:} \quad &
    c_j(i) = \frac{x_{ij}-\min_j}{\max_j-\min_j}, \label{eq:mm} \\
  \text{vector:} \quad &
    c_j(i) = x_{ij} \big/ \lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_2, \label{eq:vec} \\
  \text{ranks:} \quad &
    c_j(i) = r_j(i). \label{eq:ranks}
\end{align}
Cost criteria are complemented in~\eqref{eq:mm}; ranks are midranks with
ties averaged.
Two properties drive our results.
First, none of the three is invariant to the inclusion or exclusion of
alternatives: min--max depends on the sample extrema, vector normalisation
on the sample norm, and ranks on the sample composition.
Second, the choice interacts with the aggregation: VIKOR is defined on a
linear normalisation whereas TOPSIS is classically defined on the vector
form, a contrast noted by \citet{OpricovicTzeng2004} and implicated in
TOPSIS rank reversal \citep{GarciaCascales2012,Kuo2017}.
The reference set over which the normalisation constants are computed is
treated as an experimental factor in Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\subsection{Aggregation}

Stage~4 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. Four aggregations produce a complete ranking of the $\Nvia$ admissible
sites: SAW and TOPSIS \citep{Hwang1981}, VIKOR \citep{OpricovicTzeng2004},
and PROMETHEE~II with the usual (Type~I) preference function
\citep{Brans1986,Mareschal2008}.
In TOPSIS and VIKOR the ideal and anti-ideal points are computed on the
already oriented criterion matrix; applying the orientation a second time
inverts cost criteria and constitutes a documented implementation hazard.

\subsection{Portfolio assembly}

Stage~5 of Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}. Given a site score $s(\cdot)$, two assembly rules are compared.
\emph{Greedy}: add sites in decreasing score order, skipping any site
that violates~\eqref{eq:rsep} against those already selected.
\emph{Optimal}: maximise the additive score over the feasible set,
$\argmax_{t\in\Tset}\sum_{i\in t}s(i)$, by complete enumeration
of~$\Tset$.
Both are exact given $s$; they differ only in whether the separation
constraint is handled myopically or globally.
Greedy assembly is the implicit rule whenever a ranking is read top-down,
which is the dominant practice in the applied siting literature; optimal
assembly corresponds to the ranking-plus-integer-programming paradigm
\citep{BransMareschal1992,Mavrotas2006,VetscheraDeAlmeida2012}.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

The experiment evaluates every combination of aggregation method, normalisation
rule, and assembly strategy on the Fogo Island dataset.
This section defines the three sources of weight-vector variation, the performance
measure, and the normalisation reference set used throughout.

\subsection{Three entry points for the weight vector}

A dominance test involves weights at three distinct places, and the
distinction is not cosmetic:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $\wsc$: the weights in the \emph{method score}, which order
    the sites and hence determine the assembled portfolio $t=M(\wsc)$;
  \item $\wob$: the weights used to map the assembled portfolio to
    objective space, $\mathbf{F}(t;\wob)$;
  \item $\wfr$: the weights used to build the reference set,
    $\Fset(\wfr)$ of~\eqref{eq:front}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Coherence condition.}
\label{rem:coherence}
A dominance test is well defined only if $\wob=\wfr$.
If they differ, the portfolio and the members of the reference set are
points of different objective spaces and their componentwise comparison
has no decision-theoretic content.
We state this explicitly because it is easy to violate silently when the
reference set is precomputed once and the Monte Carlo loop recomputes
portfolio objectives per draw.

Weights are perturbed as $w\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\kappa\,\wahp)$ with
concentration $\kappa\in\{50,200\}$ and $\ndraws$ draws per cell; larger
$\kappa$ concentrates the draws around $\wahp$.
Throughout, $\kappa$ denotes only this concentration parameter; the
separation threshold is always written $d_{\min}=\dmin$~km.

\subsection{Performance measures and pre-specification}

The experiment evaluates each method--normalisation--assembly cell by
two performance measures.
The \emph{primary} measure is
\begin{equation}
  Q_3 = \mathbb{E}_{w}\bigl[\,\mathrm{gap}(M(w),w)\,\bigr],
    \qquad \wsc=\wob=\wfr=w, \label{eq:Q3}
\end{equation}
the expected optimality gap~\eqref{eq:gap}, which is continuous on
$[0,1]$ and retains information about how far below the frontier the
assembled portfolio falls.
The \emph{secondary} measure is
\begin{equation}
  Q_2 = \Pr_{w}\bigl[\,M(w)\in\Fset(w)\,\bigr],
    \qquad \wsc=\wob=\wfr=w. \label{eq:Q2}
\end{equation}
$Q_2$ is the probability of reaching the frontier; $Q_3=0$ if and only if
$Q_2=1$.
Both are averaged over $\ndraws$ draws per cell.
The cell SAW/min--max/optimal satisfies $Q_2=1$ and $Q_3=0$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt}; it serves as a regression test and is
excluded from substantive comparisons.

\paragraph{Pre-specification.}
We pre-specify the following before examining results.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep]
  \item \textbf{Primary outcome}: $Q_2=\Pr[M(w)\in\Fset(w)]$.
    Secondary: $Q_3=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{gap}]$.
  \item \textbf{Equivalence margin}: $\delta=2.5$~pp, defined as the
    smallest difference in $Q_2$ (or $Q_3$ scaled to pp) judged
    practically relevant for site-selection decisions.
    The aggregation contrast is declared \emph{equivalent} if the
    $95\%$ bootstrap CI lies entirely within $(-\delta,+\delta)$.
  \item \textbf{Minimum detectable effect}: with $\ndraws=500$
    draws per cell, empirical inter-cell correlation $\hat\rho\approx0.5$
    (paired by draw), and $Q_2\approx0.6$, the two-sided MDE at $80\%$
    power is $5.8$~pp.
    The aggregation contrast of $+0.9$~pp lies well below this
    threshold; the TOST equivalence test is therefore the appropriate
    inferential frame.
  \item \textbf{Bootstrap pairing}: replicates are paired by draw within
    each cell so that weight-vector noise cancels in contrasts.
    The pairing gain is $\hat\rho\approx0.5$, equivalent to a
    $2\times$ reduction in the variance of each contrast.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Normalisation reference set}

Min-max and vector constants may be computed over all $\Ncand$ candidates
(space~$A$) or over the $\Nvia$ admissible ones (space~$A'$).
Space~$A$ reproduces the source study ($|\Fset|=\FAsize$ portfolios);
space~$A'$ is the factorial design ($|\Fset|=\Fmmsize$), and the two
reference sets share only $\FAinter$ portfolios.
Including inadmissible sites shifts the scale without those sites being
selectable, a set-dependence mechanism documented for TOPSIS by
\citet{GarciaCascales2012}.

\subsection{Factorial design}

The main experiment is a balanced $2^3$: aggregation
$\{\text{SAW},\text{TOPSIS}\}$ $\times$ normalisation
$\{\text{min--max},\text{vector}\}$ $\times$ assembly
$\{\text{greedy},\text{optimal}\}$, evaluated in $Q_2[A']$.
A wider design adds VIKOR, PROMETHEE~II and rank normalisation.
Of the $\Ncells$ nominal combinations, two are redundant because VIKOR
is invariant to vector normalisation and four because PROMETHEE~II with
the usual preference function is ordinal and therefore identical under
all three normalisations, leaving $\Ncellsnet$ estimable cells
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exponent});
it is unbalanced and is reported only for marginal means.
Effects are reported in percentage points with $\nboot$-replicate
bootstrap intervals, paired by draw.

\section{Normalisation as implicit re-weighting: a closed-form analysis}
\label{sec:propositions}

The central analytical result of the paper is stated and derived in this section.
It takes the form of a lemma that expresses effective weights in closed form,
followed by three propositions that characterise the specific distortions
produced by min--max, vector, and rank normalisation.

% ----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The amplitude lemma}
\label{sec:amplitude}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection*{The amplitude identity}
\label{sec:amp_identity}
\label{lem:amplitude}
The following states in closed form a qualitative relationship established
by \citet{Choo1999}: a weight cannot be interpreted independently of the
scale and range of its criterion.
Let $n_j : \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^N$ be a normalisation applied
column-wise to criterion $j$, and let
\begin{equation}
  A_j = \max_i n_j(x_{ij}) - \min_i n_j(x_{ij})
\end{equation}
be the \emph{amplitude} of the normalised column.
In any additive aggregation $s(i) = \sum_j w_j n_j(x_{ij})$, the
contribution of criterion $j$ to score differences between any two
alternatives is proportional to $w_j A_j$.
The effective weights are therefore
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weff}
  w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{w_j A_j}{\sum_k w_k A_k},
\end{equation}
and $w^{\mathrm{eff}} = w$ for all $w$ if and only if $A_j$ is constant
across $j$.

\textit{Derivation.}
For any two alternatives $i$ and $i'$,
$s(i) - s(i') = \sum_j w_j [n_j(x_{ij}) - n_j(x_{i'j})]$.
The maximum absolute contribution of criterion $j$ to this difference,
over all pairs $(i,i')$, is $w_j A_j$.
The share of total score variation attributable to criterion $j$ is
therefore $w_j A_j / \sum_k w_k A_k$, which is $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}$
in~\eqref{eq:weff}.
The ``if and only if'' follows because $w^{\mathrm{eff}} = w$ iff
$w_j A_j / \sum_k w_k A_k = w_j$ for all $j$, which holds iff all
$A_j$ equal the same constant.\hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Scope and empirical confirmation.}
\label{rem:scope}
The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} is exact for additive aggregation.
Within that scope the prediction is borne out: across SAW cells,
mean $Q_2[\mathcal{A}']$ at $\kappa=200$ is $82.3\%$, $76.5\%$ and
$55.5\%$ for min--max, ranks and vector normalisation respectively,
against predicted distortions $\mathrm{TV}=0$, $0.091$ and $0.315$,
a strictly monotone ordering ($R^2=0.995$ on three means; we report the
ordering and note approximate proportionality, given $n=3$, $\mathrm{df}=1$).

TOPSIS and VIKOR aggregate distances to ideal points rather than
weighted sums, so amplitudes enter quadratically or in ratio form and
the closed form does not transfer.
Two consequences are observable.
First, VIKOR is invariant to vector normalisation because the common
scale factor cancels in its ratio form; the VIKOR$\times$vector cell is
therefore redundant and excluded from the design.
Second, pooling all aggregation families destroys the monotone ordering
precisely as the scope restriction predicts.
We read this as corroboration of the mechanism rather than as a
limitation of it: the distortion acts through the weighted sum, and
it is visible exactly where a weighted sum is taken.

\subsection{Amplitude under the three standard normalisations}
\label{sec:three_norms}
\label{cor:three}

A natural measure of aggregate distortion is the total variation
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:TV}
  \mathrm{TV} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_j |w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} - w_j|,
\end{equation}
which is invariant to multiplication of all $A_j$ by a common constant,
since that constant cancels in the renormalisation of~\eqref{eq:weff}.
Any faithful measure of re-weighting must inherit this invariance;
$D = \sum_j w_j |A_j - 1|$ does not, and is therefore not an
appropriate summary of the distortion.
The amplitude takes the following form in each of the three families.

\textbf{1) Min--max.}\;
$A_j \equiv 1$ by construction; hence $w^{\mathrm{eff}} = w$.
Min--max is the unique member of the three that preserves elicited
weights exactly under additive aggregation.

\textbf{2) Vector.}\;
$A_j = \rho_j = (\max_j x_j - \min_j x_j) / \lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_2$,
which depends on the shape of the marginal distribution.
Criteria with heavy tails obtain $\rho_j$ close to unity;
criteria with most mass at the median obtain small $\rho_j$.

\textbf{3) Midranks scaled by $N+1$.}\;
$A_j = (r_j^{\max} - r_j^{\min})/(N+1)$, which approaches~$1$
when ties are absent but contracts with tie mass.
For a binary criterion with $n_0$ zeros and $n_1 = N-n_0$ ones,
the midranks of the two groups are $(1+n_0)/2$ and $(n_0+1+N)/2$,
giving
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Arank_binary}
  A_j = \frac{N}{2(N+1)} \approx \frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
The result is \emph{independent of $n_1$}: every binary criterion
loses approximately half its nominal weight in rank space, whatever the
class balance.
A binary indicator contributes half the amplitude of a continuous
criterion with no ties.\hfill$\square$

\noindent\textbf{Empirical calibration.}
Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} reports $A_j$ for the three normalisations on
the $\Nvia$ Fogo Island sites, along with the total variation
$\mathrm{TV} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_j |w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} - w_j|$ as a
summary statistic of aggregate distortion.
Min--max gives $\mathrm{TV} = 0$ by construction.
The vector normalisation gives $\mathrm{TV} = 0.315$: criterion
\textit{perm} dominates, with $\rho_j = 0.583$ (factor
$\mathbf{2.88\times}$ relative to nominal weight) because its raw
distribution has 350 exact zeros and a maximum of~532~m, giving a
long-tailed norm.
The rank normalisation gives $\mathrm{TV} = 0.091$: \textit{perm} is
deflated ($A_j = 0.547$, factor $\mathbf{0.616\times}$) because 350
tied zeros compress the maximum midrank to $\approx 214.5$ instead
of~$388$, while \textit{aspect} (binary) gives $A_j = N/[2(N+1)]
\approx 0.499$ (factor $\mathbf{0.562\times}$) as predicted by
\eqref{eq:Arank_binary}, independent of $n_1$.
The seven fully continuous criteria are conversely \emph{inflated}
(factor $\approx 1.12\times$) because the mean amplitude $\bar{A}<1$
makes each criterion's unit relative weight larger; \textit{perm} and
\textit{aspect} lose weight, all others gain $12\%$.
The directions are reversed: the same heavy-zero distribution inflates
\textit{perm} under vector normalisation and deflates it under rank
scaling.
Figure~\ref{fig:binary_amplitude} illustrates the binary case analytically.

\subsection{Pareto consistency of optimal linear aggregation}
\label{prop:p1}

\begin{proposition}[Pareto consistency of optimal linear aggregation]
\label{prop:p1:stmt}
Let $s(a)=\sum_j w_j v_j(a)$ be the SAW score built on the same weights
and the same normalisation as \eqref{eq:F1}--\eqref{eq:F3}, with
$w_j>0$ for all $j$.
Then $T^{*}=\argmax_{T\in\Tset}\sum_{a\in T}s(a)$ satisfies
$T^{*}\in\Fset$, i.e., $Q_2=1$ almost surely.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:scalarisation},
$\sum_{a\in T}s(a)=F_1(T)+F_2(T)+F_3(T)$, so $T^{*}$ maximises a
strictly positive scalarisation of the three objectives over $\Tset$.
A maximiser of a strictly positive scalarisation is non-dominated: if
$T'$ dominated $T^{*}$ then $F_\ell(T')\ge F_\ell(T^{*})$ for all
$\ell$ with at least one strict inequality, whence
$\sum_\ell F_\ell(T')>\sum_\ell F_\ell(T^{*})$, contradicting
optimality.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The $Q_2=1$ cell is a regression test, not evidence]
\label{rem:definitional}
Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt} holds for \emph{any} normalisation,
provided the normalisation used to compute $F_\ell$ matches the one in
$s$.
The $Q_2=1$ cell therefore holds by construction and carries no
evidential weight about min--max in particular; we use it solely as a
regression test, and any implementation returning $Q_2<1$ there is
faulty.
Its informative content is the \emph{contrapositive}: every cell falling
below $100\%$ does so because some premise --- matching normalisation,
additivity, or global assembly --- fails, and the factorial identifies
which.
\end{remark}

\noindent\textbf{Empirical verification.}
SAW with min--max normalisation over the $\Nvia$ viable sites:
$Q_2[\mathcal{A}'] = 100.0\%$ for both $\kappa \in \{50, 200\}$
($\ndraws$ draws each).

\subsection{The amplitude exponent: extension beyond additive aggregation}
\label{sec:exponent}

The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} is exact for additive aggregation.
The families evaluated here differ in how the normalised column enters the
score, and the difference is captured by a single exponent.

\begin{proposition}[Amplitude exponent]
\label{prop:exponent}
Consider an aggregation that assigns to criterion $j$ a contribution
homogeneous of degree $\alpha$ in the product $w_j\,n_j(\cdot)$, where
$n_j$ is the normalised column.
The effective weights satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weff_alpha}
  w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}(\alpha)
   = \frac{w_j^{\alpha} A_j^{\alpha}}
          {\sum_k w_k^{\alpha} A_k^{\alpha}},
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{TV}(\alpha)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_j|w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}(\alpha)-w_j|$.
The three families instantiate $\alpha$ as follows.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{SAW}: $\alpha=1$.  Recovers~\eqref{eq:weff}.
  \item \textbf{TOPSIS}: $\alpha=2$, because
    $(d^{\pm})^2=\sum_j w_j^2\bigl(v_j-v_j^{\pm}\bigr)^2$ and the
    square root is monotone so it does not affect rankings.
    \emph{Scope: the exponent interpretation is by analogy with the
    additive case; the effective weight in~\eqref{eq:weff_alpha} is
    exact for the squared distance, not the distance itself.}
  \item \textbf{VIKOR, PROMETHEE~II (usual criterion)}: $\alpha=0$.
    A common scale factor cancels in VIKOR's ratio form; the usual
    preference function is ordinal and invariant to any monotone
    transformation, so amplitude is irrelevant.
\end{itemize}
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[Min--max is not amplitude-neutral for TOPSIS]
\label{cor:mm_not_neutral}
Under $\alpha=2$ and $A_j\equiv1$, \eqref{eq:weff_alpha} gives
$w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}\propto w_j^{2}$, so the total variation
$\mathrm{TV}(2)=0.160$ on the Fogo weights.
Min--max preserves elicited weights only under additive aggregation;
combining it with TOPSIS still re-weights criteria, by squaring.
\end{corollary}

$\alpha\in\{1,2\}$ and all three normalisations.

\paragraph{Empirical test of the exponent.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:exponent} predicts that TOPSIS inherits a larger
distortion than SAW, by an amount increasing in
$\Delta\mathrm{TV}=\mathrm{TV}(2)-\mathrm{TV}(1)$.
Restricting to optimal assembly, where no combinatorial loss from the
greedy heuristic confounds the contrast (Section~\ref{sec:greedy}),
$Q_2[\mathcal{A}']$ falls from SAW to TOPSIS by $3.4$~pp under min--max
($\Delta\mathrm{TV}=0.160$) and $4.4$~pp under vector
($\Delta\mathrm{TV}=0.360$): both negative and ordered as predicted.
Under greedy assembly the contrast reverses under min--max ($+7.8$~pp),
which we attribute to the foreclosure loss of Section~\ref{sec:greedy}
dominating a distortion of~$0.160$; we therefore report the
optimal-assembly cells as the test of the exponent and the greedy cells
as a description of practice, in parallel with the scope restriction
of Section~\ref{sec:lemmaconf}.

\subsection{PROMETHEE~II with usual criterion equals rank-weighted SAW}
\label{prop:p2}
Let $P_j(d) = \mathbf{1}[d > 0]$.
Denoting by $r_j(a)$ the midrank of site $a$ on criterion $j$, the
PROMETHEE~II net flow satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p2identity}
  \phi(a) = \frac{n+1}{n-1} - \frac{2}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^m w_j\, r_j(a),
\end{equation}
for any tied-value pattern.
The ranking induced by $\phi$ is therefore identical to that of
$\tilde{s}(a) = \sum_j w_j r_j(a)$.

\textit{Derivation.}
For site $a$ and criterion $j$, let $B_j(a)$ count strictly worse sites
and $T_j(a)$ be the tied-block size.
The midrank satisfies $2r_j(a) = 2B_j(a) + T_j(a) + 1$.
The net PROMETHEE flow on criterion $j$ is
$\phi_j(a) = [B_j(a) - (n - B_j(a) - T_j(a))]/(n-1)
            = [2r_j(a) - 1 - n]/(n-1)$.
Summing with weights and simplifying gives~\eqref{eq:p2identity}.\hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Rank scaling is not innocuous.}
\label{rem:p2normalization}
Dividing midranks by $N+1$, the conventional rescaling to $(0,1)$,
does not equalise criterion amplitudes when ties are massive.
With $350$ tied zeros in \texttt{perm} among $\Nvia$ sites, its maximum
midrank is $\approx 214.5$, so the rescaled column spans
$[0.003,\,0.550]$ while every other criterion spans $[0.003,\,0.997]$.
By \eqref{eq:Arank_binary}, the effective weight of
\texttt{perm} is scaled by $\approx 0.616$, and the exact identity
\eqref{eq:p2identity} no longer holds for the implemented score.
This is the rank-space counterpart of the vector-normalisation
distortion of Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}(ii), with the notable
difference that \texttt{perm} is \emph{deflated} ($\times 0.616$) in
rank space but \emph{inflated} ($\times 2.88$) in vector space: the
same heavy-zero distribution distorts in opposite directions under the
two normalisations.

\noindent\textbf{Empirical verification.}
On the Fogo Island dataset ($n = \Nvia$, $m = 11$ criteria), the
maximum absolute difference between $\phi(a)$ and
$-(2/(n-1))\tilde{s}(a)$ across all sites is $6.7 \times 10^{-16}$
(machine precision), with Spearman $\rho = 1.000000$ and zero ranking
inversions.

\subsection{Vector normalisation distorts effective weights}
\label{prop:p3}
Under vector normalisation, $v_j(a) = x_j(a) / \lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_2$.
The amplitude is $A_j = \rho_j$ as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:amp_identity}, and by~\eqref{eq:weff}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weff_vec}
  w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{w_j\,\rho_j}{\sum_k w_k\,\rho_k},
  \qquad
  \rho_j = \frac{\max_a x_j(a) - \min_a x_j(a)}
                {\lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_2}.
\end{equation}
For binary criteria, $\rho_j = 1/\sqrt{n_1}$ exactly, where $n_1$ is
the number of ones.

\textit{Derivation.}
The range of $v_j(a)$ over all sites is
$(\max_a x_j(a) - \min_a x_j(a)) / \lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_2 = \rho_j$,
so $A_j = \rho_j$ and~\eqref{eq:weff_vec} follows from~\eqref{eq:weff}.
For binary $x_j \in \{0,1\}$ with $n_1$ ones,
$\lVert\mathbf{x}_j\rVert_2 = \sqrt{n_1}$ and
$\max - \min = 1$, giving $\rho_j = 1/\sqrt{n_1}$.\hfill$\square$

\noindent\textbf{Empirical calibration.}
Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} lists $\rho_j$ and $w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}/w_j$
for all eleven criteria.
\textit{Perm} ($w_j = 0.152$): $\rho_j = 0.583$, effective-weight
factor $\mathbf{2.88\times}$.
\textit{Urban} ($w_j = 0.166$): factor $\mathbf{0.53\times}$.
\textit{Aspect} (binary, $n_1 = 94$): $\rho_j = 1/\sqrt{94} = 0.103$,
factor $0.51\times$.

\subsection{Magnitude is not enough: the concentration of the distortion}
\label{sec:concentration}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The total variation~\eqref{eq:TV} is the $\ell^1$ norm of the deviation
vector $\delta_j=w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}-w_j$, and is therefore blind to how
that deviation is spread across criteria.
Two distortions of identical $\mathrm{TV}$, one concentrated on a
single heavily weighted criterion and one spread over several, reorder
the alternatives very differently, because deviations of opposite sign
partially cancel in the weighted sum when they are distributed.
The following identity separates the two dimensions.

\begin{proposition}[Magnitude--concentration factorisation]
\label{prop:concentration}
Let $\delta_j = w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} - w_j$ be the deviation induced by a
normalisation, so that $\sum_j \delta_j = 0$ and
$\sum_j |\delta_j| = 2\,\mathrm{TV}$ by~\eqref{eq:TV}.
Define the \emph{effective number of distorted criteria} as the
participation ratio
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:meff}
  m_{\mathrm{eff}}
    = \frac{\bigl(\sum_j |\delta_j|\bigr)^2}{\sum_j \delta_j^2}
    = \frac{\lVert\delta\rVert_1^2}{\lVert\delta\rVert_2^2}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:l2fact}
  \lVert\delta\rVert_2
    = \frac{2\,\mathrm{TV}}{\sqrt{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}},
\end{equation}
and $m_{\mathrm{eff}} \in [2,\,m]$, with the lower bound attained when
exactly two criteria deviate in opposition and the upper bound when all
$|\delta_j|$ are equal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:l2fact} is immediate from~\eqref{eq:meff}:
$\lVert\delta\rVert_2 = \lVert\delta\rVert_1/\sqrt{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}$
and $\lVert\delta\rVert_1 = 2\,\mathrm{TV}$.
For the bounds, $\lVert\delta\rVert_1^2/\lVert\delta\rVert_2^2 \ge 1$
for any non-zero vector, with equality iff a single component is
non-zero; the constraint $\sum_j \delta_j = 0$ forbids that and forces
at least two non-zero components, giving
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}\ge 2$, attained at
$\delta=(c,-c,0,\dots,0)$.
The upper bound is Cauchy--Schwarz, with equality iff all $|\delta_j|$
coincide.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the factorisation buys]
\label{rem:meff_reading}
$\mathrm{TV}$ answers \emph{how much} the elicited weights were moved;
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}$ answers \emph{over how many criteria}, on a scale
whose endpoints are fixed by $m$ alone and therefore comparable across
instances.
Neither is redundant: the Euclidean norm~\eqref{eq:l2fact} is the
product of the two, and Section~\ref{sec:external} shows that it
predicts efficiency loss where $\mathrm{TV}$ alone does not.
Both are computable from the normalised decision matrix in one line, and
we recommend reporting the pair $(\mathrm{TV},\,m_{\mathrm{eff}})$
rather than $\mathrm{TV}$ alone (Section~\ref{sec:practice}).
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Fogo Island.}
Computing~\eqref{eq:meff} on the deviations implied by
Table~\ref{tab:amplitude} under vector normalisation gives
$\mathrm{TV}=0.3146$, $\lVert\delta\rVert_2=0.3127$,
$\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty=0.2859$ (attained at \textit{perm}), and
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}=4.05$ on the admissible range $[2,\,11]$.
The distortion is thus not merely large but \emph{concentrated}: a
single criterion carries $45.4\%$ of the total absolute deviation.
This replaces the qualitative observation that \textit{perm} is an
adversarial column with a position on a bounded scale.

\subsection{Consequences for the factorial comparison}
\label{sec:consequences}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} and
Sections~\ref{prop:p1}--\ref{prop:p3} partition the sources of
sub-optimality in the factorial experiment into four ordered factors:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Normalisation effect (min--max vs.\ others):}
    Min--max is the only normalisation that preserves elicited weights
    in additive aggregations (Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}).
    Under SAW, where the Lemma applies exactly, both the greedy and
    optimal assembly benefit from the preservation.
  \item \emph{Assembly effect (optimal vs.\ greedy):}
    The greedy procedure may be Pareto-dominated even when the score
    is well-calibrated (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    The assembly effect is amplified under min--max because the score
    is well-aligned with the Pareto front, and suppressed under vector
    normalisation because the distorted score is what is being
    optimised.
  \item \emph{Aggregation effect (SAW vs.\ TOPSIS vs.\ VIKOR):}
    TOPSIS and VIKOR aggregate through ideal-point distances;
    The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} does not apply exactly to them.
    The factorial experiment measures the residual effect after
    controlling for items~1 and~2.
  \item \emph{Reference-set effect (normalisation over $N$ vs.\ $N'$):}
    Including inadmissible sites in the normalisation reference shifts
    the scale without those sites being selectable
    (Section~\ref{sec:refset}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Confirmation of the amplitude lemma}
\label{sec:lemmaconf}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} predicts that performance degrades with the
distortion the normalisation imposes on the elicited weights, measured
on the simplex by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:TV_results}
  \mathrm{TV} = \tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl|w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}-w_j\bigr|,
  \qquad
  w_j^{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{w_j A_j}{\sum_k w_k A_k}.
\end{equation}
Total variation is the appropriate measure because, as noted after
Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}, $w^{\mathrm{eff}}$ is invariant to a common
rescaling of all amplitudes: a factor $c>0$ applied to every $A_j$
cancels in~\eqref{eq:TV_results} and produces no re-weighting.

Table~\ref{tab:tvorder} pairs the predicted distortion with observed
performance, restricted to additive aggregation, the scope in which
the lemma is exact.

The ordering is strictly monotone in the predicted direction, and the
magnitudes are approximately proportional: a distortion of $0.091$
costs $5.8$~pp, while $0.315$ costs $26.8$~pp.
A least-squares fit over the three levels gives a slope of
$-85.1$~pp per unit of total variation with $R^2=0.995$.
We report the \emph{ordering and approximate proportionality}, not a
predictive coefficient: with three levels the residual degree of freedom
is one, and any monotone triple admits a close linear fit.
The claim we make is the analytic one of Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}:
min--max is the unique weight-preserving choice among the three,
for which the empirical ordering is corroboration rather than
evidential foundation.

Two entries of Table~\ref{tab:tvorder} deserve comment because they
isolate distinct mechanisms acting on the same criterion.
Under vector normalisation, \texttt{perm} is \emph{inflated} to
$2.88\times$ its nominal weight, because its long right tail
concentrates the Euclidean norm in few sites.
Under midranks scaled by $N+1$, the same criterion is \emph{deflated}
to $\approx0.616\times$, because its 350 tied zeros compress the
attainable rank range to $[0.003,\,0.550]$ while every other criterion
spans $[0.003,\,0.997]$.
The two normalisations distort the same criterion in opposite
directions, for reasons the lemma makes explicit and neither
normalisation makes visible to the analyst.

have already been fixed by the normalisation.
The distortion occurs upstream of the aggregation operator, so replacing
one operator with another cannot recover it.
The applied siting literature routinely compares aggregation methods
while leaving the normalisation implicit; on this evidence that is a
comparison of the least consequential design choice.

\paragraph{Why pooling aggregation families obscures the mechanism.}
\label{rem:pooling}
Averaging $Q_2$ across all aggregation families destroys the monotone
ordering of Table~\ref{tab:tvorder}, and does so for reasons internal to
the mechanism.
VIKOR normalises by ratios to the ideal and anti-ideal point, in which
a common scale factor cancels; VIKOR is therefore invariant to vector
normalisation, the VIKOR$\times$vector cell duplicates
VIKOR$\times$min--max, and pooling it inflates the vector average
by~$5.9$~pp.
Rank normalisation under distance-based aggregation degrades performance
far more than the additive re-weighting accounts for, because ranks
distort the Euclidean geometry on which those methods depend.
Both effects are outside the scope of the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} and
both are predicted by it as exclusions.
We therefore report the additive cells as the test of the mechanism and
the full factorial as a description of practice.

\subsection{Normalisation \texorpdfstring{$\times$}{x} assembly:
  optimisation amplifies the distortion}
\label{sec:interaction}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The assembly main effect of $+16.0$~pp averages two very different
regimes.
Under min--max normalisation, replacing greedy with optimal assembly is
worth $+29.8$~pp $[+27.4,+32.2]$; under vector normalisation it is
worth $+2.2$~pp $[-0.5,+4.9]$, an interval containing zero.

The lemma explains the asymmetry.
Optimal assembly maximises $\sum_{i\in t}s(i)$ exactly, and by
Section~\ref{prop:p1} exact maximisation of an additive score yields
a Pareto-efficient portfolio \emph{under the weights that score
encodes}.
When the score encodes $w^{\mathrm{eff}}\neq w$, optimal assembly
solves the wrong problem exactly: it pursues the criterion trade-offs of
$w^{\mathrm{eff}}$, which in the vector case inflates \texttt{perm}
by $2.88\times$ and deflates \texttt{urban} to $0.53\times$.
Greedy assembly, being myopic, is partly insulated; it fails for its
own reasons, but it does not optimise the distortion.

Figure~\ref{fig:interaction} shows the pattern across all three
normalisations: the gain from optimal assembly is $+22.2$~pp under
min--max, $+15.0$~pp under ranks, and $+9.2$~pp under vector (means
averaged over aggregation methods and Monte Carlo draws).
The three lines have very different slopes, confirming that the
main-effect contrast of $+16.0$~pp conceals a structural interaction
rather than a uniform shift.

  (Table~\ref{tab:effects}) differ from these slopes because the
  model averages over both normalisations simultaneously.}
\label{fig:interaction}
\end{figure}

This has a direct implication for practice.
Adding integer programming on top of a ranking is a standard upgrade
\citep{BransMareschal1992,Mavrotas2006,VetscheraDeAlmeida2012},
but on this evidence the upgrade pays only when the normalisation
already preserves the elicited weights.
Exact assembly on a vector-normalised score captures almost none of the
available gain.
\subsection{The reference set: a second, more severe mechanism}
\label{sec:refset}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

The results of Section~\ref{sec:lemmaconf} show that Mechanism~1
(implicit re-weighting, $\mathrm{TV}$) orders the three normalisations
within additive aggregation.
A second mechanism governs the fourth cell, and it is more severe.

\paragraph{Mechanism 2: score--evaluator mismatch.}
Section~\ref{prop:p1} guarantees Pareto efficiency for the optimal
portfolio only when the \emph{score and the objectives use the same
normalisation}.
When they do not, the guarantee is not weakened but voided.
The observed cost (Table~\ref{tab:fourthcell}: $100\%\to20\%$,
a loss of $80$~pp under optimal assembly) far exceeds what
Mechanism~1 predicts: $\mathrm{TV}=0.107$ implies $\approx9.0$~pp,
accounting for only $11\%$ of the effect ($9.0/80$).
The gap is the signature of Mechanism~2.

This also explains the interaction of Section~\ref{sec:interaction}.
Under vector normalisation, exact assembly optimises a score that is
misaligned with the evaluator; the $+2.2$~pp gain is residual.
Mechanism~1 (re-weighting) and Mechanism~2 (mismatch) are additive:
mismatch removes the theoretical warrant for optimising, and re-weighting
distorts what is being optimised.

Min--max normalisation satisfies $A_j\equiv1$ over the set on which its
constants are computed.
If that set includes alternatives that cannot be selected, the guarantee
no longer applies: over the $\Nvia$ admissible sites, the attainable
amplitude becomes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:refsetA}
  A_j = \frac{\max_{\Nvia}(j)-\min_{\Nvia}(j)}
             {\max_{\Ncand}(j)-\min_{\Ncand}(j)} \;\le\; 1,
\end{equation}
which varies across criteria and re-weights (Mechanism~1 component).
Computing~\eqref{eq:refsetA} for the eleven criteria yields
$\mathrm{TV}=0.107$ (Table~\ref{tab:amprefset}).

\subsection{The greedy shortfall}
\label{sec:greedy}
% ----------------------------------------------------------

Section~\ref{prop:p1} guarantees that optimal assembly of an
additive score attains the reference set under matching weights;
Table~\ref{tab:factorial} shows greedy assembly of the same score
reaching only $64.6\%$.
The shortfall is attributable to the assembly rule alone, with
aggregation, normalisation and reference set held fixed.

Draw~$302$ ($\kappa=200$) exhibits it concretely.
The greedy portfolio is $\{184,279,320\}$ with $\mathrm{gap}=0.028$:
it is dominated by an attainable portfolio by $2.8\%$ of the achievable
range on every objective simultaneously, while optimal assembly on the
identical score and weights attains the reference set exactly.

The mechanism is the separation constraint.
Greedy commits to the highest-scoring site and then discards every site
within $d_{\min}$ of it, foreclosing combinations a global search
retains.
Because~\eqref{eq:rsep} is a hard constraint rather than an objective
\citep{Erkut1996,MoonChaudhry1984}, the foreclosure cannot be undone by
re-weighting: no choice of $w$ restores a site greedy has already
excluded.
This distinguishes the assembly effect from the normalisation effect:
the former is a combinatorial loss, the latter a re-parameterisation
of the value model.

\subsection{External validity: synthetic instances}
\label{sec:external}

The preceding sections quantify both mechanisms on a single candidate
set, Fogo Island, whose marginal distributions are specific and in one
respect extreme: \textit{perm} carries $350$ tied zeros.
This section separates the mechanism from the instance by re-drawing the
criterion columns while holding the spatial structure fixed: each
synthetic instance reuses the Fogo distance matrix, and hence the same
$|\Tset|=\Ntrios$ feasible trios, and varies only the marginals.

\paragraph{Design.}
The manipulated factor is the \emph{number} of sparse criteria rather
than a global zero mass.
The reason is analytic: $\mathrm{TV}$ is invariant to a common amplitude
level (Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}), so applying the same zero mass to
every column leaves the elicited weights untouched however extreme that
mass becomes.
Distortion requires heterogeneity \emph{across} columns.
We therefore cross $n_{\mathrm{sparse}}\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}$, with zero mass
applied to the $n_{\mathrm{sparse}}$ most heavily weighted criteria,
with $p_0\in\{0.50,0.75,0.90,0.95\}$ and two tail shapes (lognormal,
bimodal), with $6$ replications: $240$ instances at $200$ draws each.
Four cells are evaluated per instance: SAW under min--max and vector
normalisation, each with greedy and optimal assembly.
The objectives $F_\ell$ are defined by min--max normalisation
throughout, so the vector cells measure score--evaluator divergence as
well as re-weighting.
The min--max/optimal cell is the regression test of
Proposition~\ref{prop:p1:stmt} and returned $Q_2=1$ in $240/240$
instances.

\paragraph{Unit of analysis.}
Replications within a cell are not independent draws of the manipulated
factor, so inference is conducted on the $20$ cell means.
The intra-class correlation is $\mathrm{ICC}=0.35$, giving a design
effect of $4.8$ and an effective sample size of
$n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx50$ against the nominal $240$; reporting on
$240$ rows would understate the standard errors by roughly a factor
of two.

\paragraph{Total variation alone does not order the loss.}
Over all $20$ cells the loss correlates with $\mathrm{TV}$ at
$r=0.600$ ($95\%$ CI by Fisher transformation $[0.214,\,0.824]$,
$n=20$), but the relation is not monotone: $21.6\%$ of the $190$ cell
pairs are inversions (Kendall $\tau_b=0.550$).
Restricting to the $16$ cells that carry a distortion at all
($n_{\mathrm{sparse}}\ge1$), the association is statistically
indistinguishable from no effect ($r=0.346$, $p=0.189$): the
correlation over all $20$ cells is carried by the four cells in which
no criterion is sparse and the loss is zero by construction.
The inversions run in both directions of the design.
Holding $p_0$ fixed, the loss falls as the distortion is spread over
more criteria while $\mathrm{TV}$ does not fall correspondingly;
holding $n_{\mathrm{sparse}}=1$, the loss rises with $p_0$ up to
$0.90$ and then falls by $16.4$~pp at $p_0=0.95$ although
$\mathrm{TV}$ continues to rise.
Table~\ref{tab:external} shows the reversal directly.

\paragraph{The factorisation does.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:concentration} accounts for this.
Regressing the loss on the $16$ distorted cells gives
$R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.057$ for $\mathrm{TV}$ alone ($r=0.346$,
$p=0.189$, statistically indistinguishable from no effect)
against $R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.488$ for $\lVert\delta\rVert_2$
of~\eqref{eq:l2fact} ($r=0.723$, $p=0.002$).
Entering magnitude and concentration separately gives
$R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.582$ with a negative coefficient on
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}$ ($-0.026$ per effective criterion, $p=0.0008$): at
fixed $\mathrm{TV}$, spreading the distortion over more criteria
\emph{reduces} the loss, because deviations of opposite sign cancel in
the weighted sum.
The combined model, with both predictors, reaches $R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.665$,
confirming that magnitude is necessary but not sufficient: concentration
is the term that $\mathrm{TV}$ omits.

\paragraph{What the grid does not establish.}
Under the two-group amplitude model the participation
ratio~\eqref{eq:meff} depends only on the total weight of the sparse
columns, so $m_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is constant within each level of
$n_{\mathrm{sparse}}$ and takes only the four values
$\{3.59,\,6.42,\,8.38,\,9.67\}$ across the grid
($\text{Spearman }\rho(m_{\mathrm{eff}},n_{\mathrm{sparse}})=1$).
Concentration was therefore not varied orthogonally to the manipulated
factor: the model in $\mathrm{TV}$ and $m_{\mathrm{eff}}$
($R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.582$) is a reparameterisation of the model in
$\mathrm{TV}$ and $n_{\mathrm{sparse}}$ ($0.532$), and the
$5$-percentage-point gain reflects the concavity of the transformation
rather than independent information.
The factorisation~\eqref{eq:l2fact} is an algebraic identity and holds
for any deviation vector; what the grid supports is that the loss
responds to the combination of magnitude and concentration rather than
to magnitude alone, not that $m_{\mathrm{eff}}$ has been identified as
a separate causal channel.
A design in which the sparse columns carry unequal zero masses would
decouple the two, and is the natural next step.

\paragraph{Reconciliation with Fogo, and where the generator falls short.}
Fogo has $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=4.05$ on the admissible range $[2,11]$,
placing it near the $n_{\mathrm{sparse}}=1$ regime of the grid
($m_{\mathrm{eff}}=3.59$).
The model in $\lVert\delta\rVert_2$, fitted on the synthetic cells
alone, predicts $22.2$~pp at Fogo's $\lVert\delta\rVert_2=0.3127$,
with a $95\%$ prediction interval for a new instance of
$[7.5,\,36.9]$~pp, against the $29.9$~pp observed; the model in
$\mathrm{TV}$ predicts $14.0$~pp with interval $[-4.7,\,32.7]$~pp.
Both intervals contain the observed value, but the
$\lVert\delta\rVert_2$ prediction is $7.7$~pp below it while the
$\mathrm{TV}$ prediction is $15.9$~pp below, and the latter interval
extends into negative losses, which are not attainable.
Fogo's $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=4.05$ lies between the two lowest values
sampled by the grid and $0.46$ above the nearest, within the first
spacing of $2.83$; the prediction is an interpolation, but in a
variable sampled at four levels only.

The shortfall is systematic and its source identifiable.
The generator inflates $n_{\mathrm{sparse}}$ columns and deflates the
remainder uniformly.
Fogo's deviation vector is not of that form: \textit{perm} is inflated
$2.88\times$ while \textit{urban} $(0.53\times)$, \textit{aspect}
$(0.51\times)$, \textit{aero} $(0.43\times)$ and \textit{wells}
$(0.40\times)$ are independently and unequally deflated
(Table~\ref{tab:amplitude}).
Two-sided distortion of this kind displaces the weight vector further
along the simplex than a one-sided distortion of the same
$\lVert\delta\rVert_2$, which is why the synthetic model underestimates
rather than overestimates.
We report the direction of the bias rather than correcting for it: the
synthetic grid establishes that the loss is governed by magnitude
\emph{and} concentration, and bounds Fogo from below; it does not
reproduce Fogo's particular deviation geometry.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The preceding results identify two independent mechanisms by which normalisation
affects portfolio quality.
This section interprets their relative severity, places them within the
portfolio decision analysis literature, and connects them to the broader
normalisation debate.

\subsection{Two mechanisms, ordered by severity}

The results separate two distinct ways in which a normalisation choice
degrades portfolio quality, and the distinction matters because the
remedies differ.

The first is \emph{implicit re-weighting}, characterised in closed form
by the amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff}: an additive score built on normalised
columns of unequal amplitude operates on effective weights
$w^{\mathrm{eff}}\neq w$, whatever the analyst elicited.
The effect is graded, is summarised by the total
variation~\eqref{eq:TV_results}, and orders the three normalisations
monotonically within additive aggregation (Table~\ref{tab:tvorder}).
Within Fogo the ordering is monotone in $\mathrm{TV}$
(Table~\ref{tab:tvorder}), but Section~\ref{sec:external} shows that
this does not generalise: $\mathrm{TV}$ is the $\ell^1$ norm of the
deviation and two distortions of equal $\mathrm{TV}$ differ in effect
according to their concentration.
The graded quantity is $\lVert\delta\rVert_2$, of which $\mathrm{TV}$
is one of two factors.
It is also invisible: nothing in a standard TOPSIS or SAW report reveals
that \texttt{perm} entered at $2.88$ times its nominal weight.

The second is \emph{score--evaluator mismatch}.
Section~\ref{prop:p1} guarantees Pareto efficiency for exact
maximisation of an additive score only when the score and the value
functions defining the objectives use the same normalisation.
When they do not, the guarantee is not weakened but voided, and the
observed cost exceeds what re-weighting alone predicts by a factor of
$8.9\times$ in the direction where the score loses resolution (the
$\Ncand$-score evaluated against $A'$: $80$~pp observed versus $9.0$~pp
predicted), while in the opposite direction the observed loss is smaller
than predicted ($0.56\times$: the $\Nvia$-score against $A$).
Since the total variation is symmetric between the two directions by
construction, no re-weighting measure can account for this $16\times$
asymmetry in observed loss; the two mechanisms are distinct
(Section~\ref{sec:refset}).
Two findings are attributable to this mechanism: the collapse under
reference-set misalignment (Table~\ref{tab:fourthcell}), and the
near-absence of any gain from exact assembly under vector normalisation
($+2.2$~pp, interval containing zero).

The practical ordering follows.
Re-weighting is a modelling error that can be quantified and, if
accepted, documented.
Mismatch is a specification error that removes the theoretical warrant
for optimising at all.

\subsection{Relation to portfolio decision analysis}

The closest antecedents study portfolio construction from
multi-criteria scores.
\citet{BransMareschal1992} introduced constrained selection over
PROMETHEE flows; \citet{Mavrotas2006} combined multi-criteria scores
with binary programming; \citet{VetscheraDeAlmeida2012} formulated
$c$-optimal portfolios, of which the present problem with $k=\kk$ is an
instance; and \citet{DeAlmeidaVetschera2012} showed that PROMETHEE~V
portfolios depend on the scale transformation applied to the flows.

The last of these is the nearest precedent, and the difference is
instructive.
\citet{DeAlmeidaVetschera2012} establish that scale matters for one
method's portfolios, without an external reference against which to
measure the consequence.
We give a closed form for the induced re-weighting, applicable to any
additive aggregation and any normalisation, and measure the consequence
against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto set.
The robust portfolio modelling line \citep{Liesio2007,Liesio2008} and
SMAA-PO \citep{Yang2015SMAAPO} compute non-dominated portfolios exactly, but
in a scalar value space under weight uncertainty; our reference set
retains three unaggregated partial value functions, which permits the
dominance test to detect trade-off losses that a scalar criterion cannot.

Neither line imposes a hard pairwise separation constraint.
That constraint is standard in the location literature
\citep{MoonChaudhry1984,Erkut1996}, and it is what makes the assembly
effect combinatorial rather than parametric: a site foreclosed by greedy
selection cannot be recovered by any re-weighting
(Section~\ref{sec:greedy}).

\subsection{Relation to the normalisation literature}

That criterion weights cannot be interpreted independently of the
normalisation is established.
\citet{Choo1999} show that a weight is meaningful only relative to the
scale and range of the criterion it multiplies, and distinguish nominal
from effective weights; \citet{Milani2005} demonstrate empirically that
the choice of normalisation norm changes rankings, particularly under
TOPSIS.
Set dependence has been documented for specific methods:
\citet{GarciaCascales2012} exhibit rank reversal under changes to the
alternative set, \citet{Kuo2017} identifies a failure of the closeness
index, and \citet{OpricovicTzeng2004} trace the linear-versus-vector
contrast between VIKOR and TOPSIS.

Our contribution is not the existence of the effect but its closed form
and its cost.
The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} expresses the effective weight as
$w_j^{\mathrm{eff}}\propto w_j A_j$ and identifies $A_j$ for each of
the three standard normalisations, which yields three results that the
qualitative statement does not: min--max is the unique weight-preserving
choice among them; every binary criterion loses half its weight in rank
space irrespective of class balance; and VIKOR's invariance to vector
normalisation follows immediately, since a common amplitude factor
cancels in~\eqref{eq:weff}.
Three observations previously treated separately become corollaries of
one identity: Section~\ref{prop:p3}, the rank-space contraction of
Section~\ref{sec:three_norms}, and the amplitude loss
of~\eqref{eq:refsetA}.

The second difference is evidential.
Existing comparisons measure the consequence of a normalisation choice by
agreement between rankings
\citep{Jahan2015,Aytekin2021,Vafaei2018,Vafaei2022,Salabun2020Sym},
which establishes that outcomes differ without establishing that any of
them is worse.
We measure the consequence against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto set:
a loss of $26.8$~pp in the probability of selecting a non-dominated
portfolio is a statement about decision quality, not about disagreement.

This leads us to qualify a standing recommendation.
\citet{Milani2005} advise vector normalisation on the grounds that it is
less sensitive to the scale and range of the raw data.
Our results indicate that the advantage is bought at a price:
insensitivity to unit artefacts is obtained by letting the distributional
shape of each criterion determine its amplitude, and hence its effective
weight.
Where weights carry no normative content the trade may be acceptable.
Where they encode elicited stakeholder judgements, as AHP weights do, a
normalisation that silently multiplies one criterion by $2.88\times$ and
divides another by $0.53\times$ is difficult to defend, and min--max
on the admissible set is preferable on precisely the grounds
\citet{Choo1999} articulate.

\subsection{The PROMETHEE--Borda identity}

Section~\ref{prop:p2} is a corollary of results established three
decades ago.
\citet{Bouyssou1992} characterises the net flow method axiomatically and
shows that it reduces to Copeland or Borda when the valued preference
relations are crisp; \citet{Marchant1996} extends the Borda equivalence
to valued relations.
With the usual preference function the relations are crisp by
construction, so the identity is implied.
We restate it to fix the affine constants, to record that it holds
exactly under arbitrary tie structure, and chiefly because it isolates
normalisation as the sole source of empirical divergence: in our data
the entire departure of the implemented rank score from the net flow is
attributable to rescaling midranks by $N+1$ rather than to the
aggregation.
Without the identity, that divergence would plausibly be attributed to
the method.

\subsection{Implications for practice}
\label{sec:practice}

First, normalise over the set of alternatives that can actually be
chosen, and report the set used.
Screening criteria applied after normalisation leave the value scale
calibrated on alternatives that are unavailable, which is the most
damaging configuration we observe.

Second, verify that the score and the value model share a normalisation
before adding exact optimisation.
Integer programming on a mismatched score optimises the mismatch; the
$+29.8$~pp available under min--max falls to $+2.2$~pp under vector
normalisation.

Third, report effective weights alongside elicited weights, and with
them the pair $(\mathrm{TV},\,m_{\mathrm{eff}})$
of~\eqref{eq:TV} and~\eqref{eq:meff}, which requires one line given
the normalised matrix.
The total variation alone is insufficient: it is the $\ell^1$ norm of
the deviation and is invariant to whether that deviation sits on one
criterion or on many, a distinction that
Section~\ref{sec:external} shows governs the loss.
A report stating $\mathrm{TV}=0.31$ with $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=4$ on a
scale of $[2,11]$ tells the decision maker both that the elicited
weights were substantially moved and that the movement is concentrated;
neither number conveys this alone.
Where elicited weights carry normative content, as with AHP judgements
from stakeholders, a normalisation that silently reweights them is
difficult to defend.

Comparing aggregation operators, by contrast, is the least consequential
of the design choices we examine ($+0.9$~pp, equivalent to zero within
$\pm2.5$~pp by TOST),
and the mechanism explains why: aggregation acts downstream of the
distortion.

\subsection{Limitations}

\emph{Scope of the closed form.}
The amplitude identity~\eqref{eq:weff} is exact for additive
aggregation.
Proposition~\ref{prop:exponent} extends it to distance-based and
ordinal families through the amplitude exponent, but the extension is
exact for the squared TOPSIS distance rather than the distance itself,
and we have not characterised families whose contribution is not
homogeneous in $w_j n_j(\cdot)$, outranking methods with non-linear
preference functions in particular.

\emph{Empirical support for the ordering.}
The ordering of the three normalisation levels within SAW by
$\mathrm{TV}$ matches the ordering by $Q_2$ on Fogo, providing support
for the analytic prediction within the instance; Section~\ref{sec:external}
extends the evidence across $240$ synthetic instances, where
$\lVert\delta\rVert_2$ predicts efficiency loss with
$R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.488$ against $0.057$ for $\mathrm{TV}$, though the
relation is not globally monotone across levels of the design
(Kendall $\tau_b=0.550$; Section~\ref{sec:external}).
Establishing a predictive coefficient from real datasets would require
additional normalisation families (sum, max, Weitendorf) at modest
computational cost.

\emph{Instance dependence, and what the synthetic grid settles.}
The analytic results hold for any instance; the magnitudes do not.
Section~\ref{sec:external} replaces a qualitative caveat with a
quantitative one: across $240$ synthetic instances sharing Fogo's
spatial structure, efficiency loss is governed by the concentration of
the distortion as well as its magnitude
($R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.488$ for $\lVert\delta\rVert_2$ against
$0.057$ for $\mathrm{TV}$), and Fogo's $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=4.05$ places
it at the concentrated end of the range $[2,11]$.
Two limits remain.
The synthetic generator produces one-sided distortion, with a few columns
inflated and the rest deflated uniformly, whereas Fogo's deviation
vector is two-sided, with one criterion inflated $2.88\times$ and four
independently deflated below $0.55\times$; the synthetic model
consequently underestimates Fogo's loss by $7.7$~pp and should be read
as a lower bound.
And the joint distribution of $(\mathrm{TV},m_{\mathrm{eff}})$ in
applied decision matrices is unknown; establishing it would require a
corpus of published matrices rather than a single case.

\emph{Weight perturbation model.}
Dirichlet perturbation around $\wahp$ represents symmetric uncertainty in
relative weights.
For structured disagreement among stakeholders,
SMAA-style weight-space analysis \citep{Lahdelma2002} would be
appropriate.

\emph{Regulatory grounding.}
The exclusion of the $\Nexcl$ overlapping sites is treated as a
screening decision.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =======================================================================

We evaluated ranking-based multi-criteria methods for multi-site landfill
selection against an exhaustively enumerated Pareto frontier of
$\Ntrios$ portfolios under a hard spatial separation constraint.
The design crossed aggregation, normalisation and assembly, and measured
how often each combination recovers a Pareto-efficient portfolio under
perturbation of the elicited weights.

The choice of aggregation operator, the variable the applied
literature compares most, proved the least consequential of the three:
$+0.9$~pp ($95\%$ CI $[-0.5,\,+2.3]$), statistically equivalent to
zero within the pre-specified margin $\delta=2.5$~pp (TOST).
Normalisation was worth $+29.9$~pp and assembly $+16.0$~pp.
The explanation is analytical rather than empirical: in additive
aggregation the effective weight of a criterion is proportional to
$w_j A_j$, where $A_j$ is the amplitude of the normalised column, so
the normalisation fixes the operative weights before the aggregation
operator is applied.
Min--max normalisation is the unique member of the three standard
families that leaves the elicited weights intact.
Vector normalisation lets the distributional shape of each criterion set
its weight, and rank rescaling penalises criteria with heavy tie mass;
by exactly one half for any binary indicator, irrespective of class
balance.

Two consequences bear directly on practice.
First, exact optimisation inherits the distortion: replacing greedy
assembly with complete enumeration is worth $+29.8$~pp when the score
preserves the elicited weights and $+2.2$~pp when it does not, an
interval containing zero.
Integer programming on top of a ranking, the established upgrade path in
portfolio MCDM, therefore pays only once the normalisation is consistent
with the value model.
Second, normalising over alternatives that cannot be selected, in our
data the $\Nexcl$ candidates overlapping protected areas, calibrates
the value scale on unavailable options and costs up to $80$~pp of Pareto
efficiency, considerably more than the re-weighting alone accounts for.
The loss is markedly asymmetric between the two directions of mismatch,
which indicates that scale misalignment and implicit re-weighting are
distinct mechanisms rather than two readings of one effect.

The immediate recommendation is inexpensive: report effective weights
alongside elicited ones.
Given the normalised decision matrix the computation is a single line,
and it exposes distortions that no rank-agreement diagnostic reveals.
Where weights encode stakeholder judgements, as AHP weights do, a
normalisation that silently multiplies one criterion by $2.88\times$ and
divides another by $0.53\times$ is difficult to defend.

Three limits delimit these findings.
The closed form is exact for additive aggregation; TOPSIS and VIKOR
combine distances to ideal points, where amplitudes enter quadratically
or in ratio form, and extending the characterisation to those families
remains open.
The monotone relation between predicted distortion and observed
efficiency rests on three normalisation levels, which supports an
ordering but not a predictive coefficient; adding sum, max and
Weitendorf normalisations would give the degrees of freedom required, at
modest computational cost.
And the magnitudes are specific to one candidate set: the analytical
results hold for any instance, but the size of the distortion depends on
the marginal distributions, and a criterion with $350$ tied zeros among
$\Nvia$ sites is an adversarial case.
Fogo Island quantifies the mechanism; it does not estimate how large it
typically is.

The broader implication is methodological.
Benchmarking multi-criteria methods by agreement between their rankings
can establish that they differ, but not that any is closer to the
decision maker's objectives.
Where the feasible set is enumerable, as it is for
cardinality-constrained siting problems of realistic size, an exact
frontier provides an external standard, and under that standard the
ranking of design choices is not the one the literature's emphasis would
suggest.
